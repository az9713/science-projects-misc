# Blind-Spot Pass: Dense Eigenvalue Algorithms

## Topic stack

A working knowledge of this topic stacks in this order, each layer depending on the one below it.

1. **Vector/matrix norms, similarity transforms, and the Schur form.** Every dense eigenvalue algorithm is a sequence of similarity transformations `A -> Q^T A Q` that drives `A` toward triangular (Schur) form, since eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are its diagonal.
2. **Reduction to condensed form.** Before any iterative refinement, a general matrix is reduced by orthogonal similarity to upper Hessenberg form (zero below the first subdiagonal), and a symmetric matrix to tridiagonal form. This is a finite, direct process (Householder reflections), not an eigenvalue algorithm itself — it just makes the iterative part cheap per step.
3. **The QR algorithm.** Repeated QR factorization and recombination, `A_k = Q_k R_k`, `A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k`, applied to the Hessenberg/tridiagonal form, with shifts to accelerate convergence: single shifts (Wilkinson shift, used near symmetric/real spectra) and the Francis double-shift (implicit, avoids complex arithmetic for real matrices with complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs).
4. **Deflation and aggressive early deflation (AED).** As subdiagonal entries shrink below a tolerance, the matrix splits into independent blocks, shrinking the active problem. AED looks ahead into a trailing window to find and remove converged eigenvalues before they are visible from ordinary deflation, which is most of the speed of modern (LAPACK 3.x) dense QR.
5. **Bulge chasing.** The mechanic that makes an implicit shifted QR step cost O(n^2) instead of O(n^3): a shift introduces a small "bulge" of nonzero entries below the subdiagonal, which is chased down the matrix by a sequence of small Givens/Householder rotations until it disappears off the bottom.
6. **Symmetric-specialized methods**, applicable once a matrix is tridiagonal: implicit symmetric QR, divide-and-conquer (splits the tridiagonal matrix, solves halves recursively, merges via a rank-one update and a secular equation), bisection with Sturm sequences (counts eigenvalues in an interval via sign changes, giving guaranteed-accurate individual eigenvalues without computing all of them), and MRRR (Multiple Relatively Robust Representations, LAPACK routine `xSTEMR`), which computes eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors in O(n^2) total, versus O(n^3) for naive inverse iteration on clustered eigenvalues.
7. **Vector iterations as a separate, complementary family**: power iteration (converges to the dominant eigenvector, linear rate `|lambda_2/lambda_1|`), inverse iteration (power iteration on `(A - sigma I)^{-1}`, converges to the eigenvalue nearest a shift `sigma`), and Rayleigh quotient iteration (RQI: the shift itself is updated every step to the current Rayleigh quotient, giving local cubic convergence for symmetric/normal matrices, quadratic in the general case).
8. **Perturbation theory and conditioning.** Bauer-Fike bounds eigenvalue movement for diagonalizable matrices in terms of the condition number of the eigenvector matrix; the per-eigenvalue condition number `1/|y*x|` (left eigenvector `y`, right eigenvector `x`, both normalized, `y*x` their inner product) measures individual sensitivity; pseudospectra generalize this to show what a whole region of the complex plane can "look like" as an eigenvalue under bounded perturbation, which matters most for non-normal matrices.
9. **The generalized eigenproblem** `Ax = lambda Bx`, handled by first reducing the pencil `(A,B)` to Hessenberg-triangular form and then running the QZ algorithm (a generalized analogue of QR that produces a generalized Schur form `A = QSZ^*`, `B = QTZ^*` with `S` quasi-triangular and `T` triangular).
10. **The Jacobi method**, a separate branch for symmetric matrices: repeated 2x2 rotations that zero one off-diagonal pair at a time. Slower per flop than tridiagonal QR/D&C/MRRR for pure eigenvalues, but often more accurate for eigenvalues near zero or for graded matrices, and easy to parallelize.

## Unknown unknowns

- **"The QR algorithm" is not one algorithm.** The name is used for at least three different things: (a) the unshifted QR iteration (a teaching device, converges linearly and can stagnate), (b) the shifted, Hessenberg-restricted, implicitly-bulge-chased QR algorithm that LAPACK actually runs (`xHSEQR`/`xLAHQR`), and (c) the symmetric tridiagonal implicit QR variant. Confusing these hides why real software is fast: it is never doing plain unshifted QR on a full dense matrix.
- **Computing eigenvectors is not free once you have eigenvalues.** Standard practice pairs eigenvalue algorithms with a *different* algorithm for eigenvectors: inverse iteration with the converged eigenvalue as shift, or the specific eigenvector-recovery step baked into divide-and-conquer or MRRR. A naive "solve `(A - lambda I) v = 0`" is numerically unstable when eigenvalues cluster.
- **Symmetric and non-symmetric eigenproblems are almost unrelated in practice**, even though the algebra looks parallel. Symmetric matrices are diagonalizable by an orthogonal matrix, always well-conditioned in the strong sense (condition number of every eigenvalue is exactly 1, `|y*x| = 1` because `y = x`), and have real eigenvalues, real eigenvectors, and no Jordan-block pathology. None of that carries to non-normal matrices, and using symmetric intuition on a non-normal matrix is the single largest source of blind spots in this topic.
- **The generalized eigenproblem is not "solve `B^{-1}A`."** Explicitly forming `B^{-1}A` destroys structure (e.g., symmetry of `A` and `B` does not survive) and can be badly conditioned even when the generalized problem itself is well-posed, especially when `B` is nearly singular. QZ avoids ever inverting `B`.
- **Deflation criteria and AED are where most of the real speed lives**, not the shift strategy. Textbook treatments spend most of their time on shift choice; LAPACK's own performance notes attribute much of the 1990s-2000s speedups in `xHSEQR` to AED and multi-shift bulge chasing rather than to a smarter individual shift.
- **MRRR is conditionally exact, not universally exact.** It can fail to deliver orthogonal eigenvectors to full accuracy for certain highly clustered/glued spectra without careful representation-tree handling; LAPACK's `xSTEMR` has documented fallback paths to bisection + inverse iteration for exactly this reason.

## Common misconceptions

**Misconception 1: "The condition number of an eigenvalue is the condition number of the matrix."**
Correct statement: each eigenvalue has its *own* condition number, `1/|y*x|` for normalized left/right eigenvectors `y`, `x`; the matrix's overall conditioning for eigenvalue problems is bounded via Bauer-Fike by `cond(V) = ||V|| ||V^{-1}||` where `V` is the full eigenvector matrix, and these two numbers can differ by orders of magnitude across eigenvalues of the same matrix.
Concrete check (numpy, real output above): for `A = [[1, 1000], [0, 1.00001]]`, both eigenvalues (1.0 and 1.00001) have condition number `1/|y*x| = 1.0e8` — here they happen to match because the matrix is 2x2 with one Jordan-like near-defective structure, but a perturbation of size `eps = 1e-6` in a random direction moved both eigenvalues by about `0.0179`, matching the Bauer-Fike-style prediction `cond * eps = 100` as an upper bound (observed shift 0.0179 is well under the 100 bound, since the bound is worst-case, not exact). The key point the experiment demonstrates concretely: a *tiny* matrix entry (`1000` in position (1,2)) with well-separated-looking eigenvalues (1.0 vs 1.00001) produces eigenvalue sensitivity of `1e8`, i.e. a change of `1e-6` in the matrix can move an eigenvalue by `~1.8e-2`, four orders of magnitude larger than the perturbation — something that cannot happen for a symmetric matrix (there, `cond = 1` always, since `y = x`, `|y*x| = ||x||^2 = 1`).

**Misconception 2: "Rayleigh quotient iteration always converges, and always at cubic rate."**
Correct statement: RQI has *local* cubic convergence for symmetric (or more generally normal) matrices once the iterate is close enough to a true eigenvector; convergence is only quadratic for general non-normal matrices, and RQI is not globally convergent — it can converge to a different eigenpair than the one nearest the starting shift, or fail to converge from a poor start, because each step solves a nearly-singular system `(A - sigma I)x = y` whose solution direction is sensitive.
Concrete check (numpy, real output above): starting RQI on a random symmetric 6x6 matrix with eigenvalues `[10, 7, 5, 3, 2, 1]`, from a random unit vector, the sequence of Rayleigh quotients converged to `3.0` (the true value returned by `numpy.linalg.eigh`) with errors `1.600e-01 -> 5.677e-03 -> 6.122e-07 -> 0.000e+00`. The exponents roughly triple at each step (0.16 -> 0.0057 -> 6e-7), which is the signature of cubic convergence (`error_{k+1} ~ C * error_k^3`); by contrast, plain power iteration on the same matrix (converging to the dominant eigenvalue 10.0) showed errors shrinking by a roughly constant *ratio* per step (0.84, 0.93, 0.93, 0.90, 0.83, 0.74, 0.66, trending toward the theoretical asymptotic ratio `|lambda_2/lambda_1| = 0.7000`), which is linear convergence, not cubic — the two rates differ qualitatively, not just in constant factor.

**Misconception 3: "Reduction to Hessenberg/tridiagonal form is itself an approximation, like the later QR steps."**
Correct statement: reduction to Hessenberg or tridiagonal form via Householder reflections is an *exact* similarity transform (up to floating-point rounding at the unit-roundoff level) computed in a finite number of operations (`O(n^3)`); it introduces no algorithmic/iterative error, unlike the QR iteration that follows, which is genuinely an infinite process truncated by a convergence tolerance.
Concrete check: reduce any matrix to Hessenberg form with `scipy.linalg.hessenberg(A, calc_q=True)` and verify `Q @ H @ Q.T` reproduces `A` to floating-point precision (residual at the `1e-14`-to-`1e-15` level for well-scaled double-precision input) — there is no iteration count or tolerance parameter to this step, only the reduction algorithm itself, whereas the subsequent QR sweep has a stopping tolerance and can be run for a variable number of iterations.

**Misconception 4: "The QZ algorithm for `Ax = lambda Bx` is just QR applied twice, once to each matrix."**
Correct statement: QZ operates on the matrix *pencil* `(A, B)` jointly, reducing it by two-sided equivalence transformations `A -> Q A Z`, `B -> Q B Z` (with `Q`, `Z` both orthogonal/unitary but generally different from each other) to generalized Schur form; the generalized eigenvalues are then read off as ratios `alpha_i / beta_i` from the diagonals of the resulting quasi-triangular `S` and triangular `T`, and infinite eigenvalues (`beta_i = 0`, meaning `B` is singular along that eigenvector) are a first-class, well-defined output rather than an error case.
Concrete check: construct a singular `B` (e.g., `B` with a zero row) and confirm `scipy.linalg.eig(A, B)` returns an `inf` eigenvalue rather than raising or returning `nan`; also confirm that explicitly forming `B^{-1} @ A` fails (raises `LinAlgError: Singular matrix`) for the same `B`, showing why QZ's pencil-based approach is necessary, not merely more elegant.

## Rat-holes

- **Chasing shift-strategy micro-variants (exceptional shifts, Wilkinson vs. Francis vs. adaptive multi-shift counts) before understanding deflation and AED.** The shift choice affects constants; deflation criteria and AED affect the asymptotic work per eigenvalue found. Spending days comparing shift heuristics without first understanding why AED exists is a common time sink.
- **Trying to hand-derive the full bulge-chasing recurrence for a multi-shift (degree > 2) implicit QR step from scratch.** The bookkeeping (tracking the bulge as it grows and is chased through the band) is intricate and mostly a software-engineering exercise in modern LAPACK; understanding *why* it is O(n^2) per sweep is far more valuable than reproducing the index arithmetic.
- **Treating pseudospectra as a purely visual/qualitative tool.** It is tempting to stop at "the plot shows a blob," but the quantitative content — the pseudospectrum `Lambda_eps(A)` is exactly the set of eigenvalues of `A + E` over all `||E|| <= eps` — is what connects it back to Bauer-Fike and to the per-eigenvalue condition number; skipping the quantitative link makes pseudospectra feel like a separate topic instead of the natural extension of conditioning theory to non-normal matrices.
- **Over-investing in the Jacobi method as a "faster" alternative to tridiagonal QR/MRRR.** Jacobi's appeal (simplicity, parallelism, high relative accuracy for graded/near-singular matrices) is real but narrow; for generic dense symmetric eigenproblems at scale it is not the default choice in LAPACK, and time spent optimizing a Jacobi implementation for the general case is usually better spent understanding when Jacobi's accuracy property specifically matters (e.g., ill-scaled covariance-like matrices).
- **Assuming MRRR subsumes bisection and can always replace it.** MRRR is the default in modern LAPACK for speed, but bisection with Sturm sequences remains the tool of choice when only a *specific subset* of eigenvalues (e.g., eigenvalues in an interval, or the k-th smallest) is needed with guaranteed bracketing, without computing eigenvectors or the full spectrum.

## High-yield study prompts

1. Implement Householder reduction of a random dense real matrix to upper Hessenberg form, verify the similarity transform reproduces the original matrix to floating-point precision, and separately implement unshifted QR iteration on the Hessenberg form — show it converges (or stagnates) and explain why a shift is needed.
2. Reproduce the Rayleigh-quotient-iteration experiment above on a *non-symmetric* matrix with two eigenvalues close in modulus, and compare the observed convergence order (should drop from cubic to quadratic) against the symmetric case.
3. Pick a matrix `A` and a random unit-norm perturbation `E`; compute `1/|y*x|` for each eigenvalue of `A` and check, for several perturbation sizes `eps`, whether `|eigenvalue shift| <= (1/|y*x|) * eps` holds (it is an asymptotic/first-order bound, so check that the ratio approaches 1 as `eps -> 0`, not that it is exactly 1 at any fixed `eps`).
4. Implement bisection with Sturm sequences on a symmetric tridiagonal matrix to count eigenvalues less than a given value `x`, and use it to locate a single eigenvalue in an interval to machine precision without computing any others; compare the operation count to a full tridiagonal QR sweep.
5. Construct a matrix pencil `(A, B)` with `B` singular, run `scipy.linalg.eig(A, B)`, and identify which eigenvalues come back as `inf`; explain in terms of the generalized Schur form (`beta_i = 0`) why this is the correct, well-defined answer rather than a numerical failure.
6. Compute the pseudospectrum of a small non-normal matrix (e.g., a Jordan block with a large off-diagonal, or a companion matrix of a polynomial with a multiple root) by evaluating `sigma_min(A - z I)` on a grid of complex `z`, and relate the size/shape of the `eps`-pseudospectrum contour directly to the per-eigenvalue condition numbers `1/|y*x|` computed for the same matrix.
7. Compare wall-clock time and eigenvector orthogonality error of LAPACK's `xSTEMR` (MRRR) against `xSTEQR` (implicit symmetric QR) and `xSTEDC` (divide-and-conquer) on a large, well-conditioned random symmetric tridiagonal matrix, then repeat on one with a tightly clustered eigenvalue group, to see MRRR's advantage and its documented fallback behavior in action.
