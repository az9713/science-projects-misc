# Blind-Spot Pass: Mixed-Precision and Hardware-Aware Algorithms in Numerical Linear Algebra

## Topic stack (what sits under what)

1. **Bit formats and their two independent budgets.** fp16 (1 sign, 5 exponent, 10 mantissa bits, unit roundoff u = 2^-11 ≈ 4.88e-4), bfloat16 (1, 8, 7 bits, u = 2^-8 ≈ 3.91e-3), fp8 variants (E4M3, E5M2 — trade mantissa for exponent range), and TF32 (19 usable bits: 8 exponent, 10 mantissa, used only inside tensor-core matmul accumulation paths). Every format has a **precision budget** (mantissa bits → u) and a **range budget** (exponent bits → overflow/underflow thresholds) that vary independently — fp16 has good precision but a narrow range (max ≈ 65504); bf16 has bad precision but fp32's range.
2. **Hardware execution model.** Tensor cores compute low-precision products with higher-precision (usually fp32) accumulation. This single fact — accumulate high, multiply low — is the hinge for almost everything above it: it is why TF32 exists, why cuBLAS/cuSOLVER and MAGMA route dense factorizations through mixed precision, and why the roofline model's arithmetic intensity changes when you change the storage format but not the accumulation format.
3. **Rounding-error analysis on top of the hardware model.** Classical backward-error bounds (Higham's *Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms*) give worst-case bounds proportional to n·u for an n-term computation. Probabilistic analysis (Higham & Mary, and related stochastic-rounding literature) gives bounds proportional to sqrt(n)·u with high probability, because worst-case bounds assume every rounding error aligns in sign, which is a measure-zero event under randomized or "generic" rounding.
4. **Algorithms built on top of both**: mixed-precision iterative refinement (2-precision and 3-precision GMRES-IR, Carson & Higham 2017/2018), low-precision LU/Cholesky used only as a preconditioner, error-free transformations (TwoSum, TwoProduct, Kahan compensated summation) that recover exactness or near-exactness from finite-precision arithmetic, and stochastic rounding as a way to make the probabilistic bound (sqrt(n)u) the *typical* case rather than the possible case.
5. **System-level layer**: communication-avoiding algorithms, the roofline model, BLAS level 1/2/3 blocking, and reproducibility of parallel reductions — this layer is where precision choice, memory movement, and numerical stability collide, because a lower-precision format is also a lower-communication-volume format (half the bytes moved per element), and reduction order (not just precision) governs reproducibility.

## Unknown unknowns (things people studying this topic usually don't know they don't know)

- **TF32 is not a storage format.** You cannot allocate a TF32 array. It is a *compute mode*: operands stored as fp32 are truncated to a 19-bit representation only during the tensor-core multiply, then accumulated in fp32. Mixing this up with "casting your array to TF32" is common and wrong.
- **GMRES-IR's convergence is a statement about three separate condition-number thresholds, not one.** Two-precision refinement (factor and solve in low precision, residual and update in higher precision, no inner solver) fails once κ(A) exceeds roughly u_factor^-1. Three-precision GMRES-IR — Carson & Higham's key result — extends the working κ(A) range to roughly u_working^-1 / u_factor by using a low-precision-factorized preconditioner inside a GMRES iteration run at working precision, with the final accuracy floor set by u_residual (the precision used to form the residual r = b − Ax). Missing that there are three independent precisions (factorization, working, residual) and three independent thresholds is the single biggest blind spot in this area.
- **Stochastic rounding does not remove bias by "adding randomness" in an unspecified way — it removes it by making E[round(x)] = x exactly.** The rounding at each step is unbiased in expectation (round up with probability equal to the fractional distance, down otherwise), which is what converts a worst-case n·u drift into a sqrt(n)·u concentrated random walk. People often know "stochastic rounding helps low-precision training" without knowing *why* it's an unbiased estimator argument, not a noise-injection regularizer argument.
- **Error-free transformations are not extended precision.** TwoSum and TwoProduct compute the *exact* rounding error of an fp32 (or fp64) addition/multiplication as a second fp32/fp64 number, using only fp32/fp64 arithmetic — no bignum or double-double library is invoked. The "compensation" step later adds that stored error back in. This is the mechanism inside Kahan summation, and it works because IEEE 754 addition/multiplication rounding error is itself exactly representable in the same format (this is a specific theorem, not a heuristic).
- **The communication-avoiding lower bound is about words moved, and it is independent of precision** — but the bytes-per-word obviously scales with precision, so halving precision literally halves the communication lower bound's byte count even though the *word*-count lower bound (a Hong–Kung / Aggarwal–Vitter style argument) is unchanged. Conflating "communication-avoiding algorithm" (fewer words moved, an algorithmic property) with "low precision" (fewer bytes per word, a data-representation property) as if they're the same lever is a frequent unknown unknown; they compose multiplicatively.
- **Reproducibility across parallel reductions is broken by *associativity*, not by rounding per se.** Floating-point addition is commutative but not associative, so `(a+b)+c ≠ a+(b+c)` in general; different reduction trees (different core counts, different scheduling) produce different but each individually-valid rounded answers. This is a property of the summation order, and it exists even at fp64 — it is not something mixed precision introduces, though lower precision makes the divergence between reduction-tree answers larger in absolute terms.
- **Probabilistic bounds are "with high probability," not "always."** The sqrt(n)u bound is a concentration-inequality statement (typically via Azuma/Hoeffding-type martingale arguments in Higham & Mary's framework) with a failure probability that must be chosen and that appears inside the bound's constant. Quoting "the error is O(sqrt(n) u)" without the associated probability and its dependence on a chosen failure tolerance is dropping a load-bearing part of the claim.

## Common misconceptions (statement, correction, concrete check)

**1. Misconception: "fp16 and bf16 differ only in how many decimal digits of precision they carry."**
Correct statement: fp16 (5 exponent bits, u = 2^-11) has *better* precision than bf16 (8 exponent bits, u = 2^-8) but a *much narrower dynamic range* (fp16 overflows above ≈ 65504; bf16 shares fp32's exponent range, roughly ±3.4e38). They are not on a single "more precise / less precise" ladder — one trades range for precision relative to the other.
Concrete check (numpy, run above): `np.array([65000.0], dtype=np.float16)` rounds to 64992.0 (rounding still works fine there), but `np.array([70000.0], dtype=np.float16)` overflows to `inf` — confirmed by direct execution, which also raised `RuntimeWarning: overflow encountered in cast`. The measured fp16 machine epsilon came out to 0.000977, matching 2^-10 = 0.0009765625 (the "gap to next representable number above 1.0," which is 2×u since u is defined as half that gap) — confirming the u = 2^-11 unit-roundoff value used throughout the mixed-precision literature.

**2. Misconception: "Mixed-precision iterative refinement always converges as long as you refine at higher precision than you factored."**
Correct statement: convergence depends on κ(A) relative to the precision *used for the factorization*, not just on refining. Standard (2-precision) refinement converges only while κ(A) ≲ u_factor^-1; push κ(A) past that and the low-precision LU factors are too inaccurate to serve as a useful correction direction, refinement stalls or diverges regardless of how high a precision you refine at. Carson–Higham's GMRES-IR (3-precision, factorization used as a *preconditioner* inside GMRES rather than as a direct correction) is specifically the fix for extending the workable κ(A) range — the three precisions have three separate roles (factorize, iterate/precondition, form residual) and the threshold on κ(A) shifts accordingly.
Concrete check (conceptual, cheap to verify without code): construct A with condition number deliberately set just above u_factor^-1 for the chosen low precision (e.g., fp16 factorization, u ≈ 4.88e-4, so κ(A) ≈ 3000–5000 already starts to stress two-precision IR); run plain 2-precision refinement and watch the residual stagnate; then wrap the same low-precision factorization as a preconditioner inside GMRES run at fp32/fp64 and observe convergence resume — the qualitative behavior (stall vs. resume) is the check, not a specific numeric threshold, since the exact crossover depends on the matrix's spectrum, not just κ(A) alone.

**3. Misconception: "Kahan summation and error-free transformations give you higher precision, as if you'd secretly switched to fp64 or fp128."**
Correct statement: they give you a *smaller constant and better scaling* on the *same*-precision error bound (roughly cutting the effective error growth from O(n·u) toward O(u), independent of n, for compensated summation of well-scaled data), not extra mantissa bits. TwoSum/Kahan track the *rounding error itself* as a second same-precision value and feed it back in; they never store more significant digits than the working format allows for any single number.
Concrete check (numpy, run above): summing n standard-normal fp32 values, naive left-to-right summation vs. Kahan summation vs. the double-precision "true" sum. At n = 1,000,000: naive error = 1.798e-2, Kahan error = 2.313e-5 — three orders of magnitude better — while both use only fp32 storage throughout (no fp64 accumulator). The naive error also roughly tracks the printed n·u = 1.192e-1 scale (same order of magnitude, well below the worst case as expected for random data), while Kahan's error stays far below even the probabilistic sqrt(n)·u = 1.192e-4 bound at that n — direct numeric evidence that compensation, not added precision, is doing the work.

**4. Misconception: "The n·u worst-case error bound is what you should expect in practice, so probabilistic sqrt(n)·u bounds are just a theoretical refinement with no practical difference."**
Correct statement: for i.i.d. or "generic" data, the n·u bound is a rare worst case requiring rounding errors to accumulate with consistent sign; typical behavior (and the behavior guaranteed with high probability under stochastic rounding) concentrates around sqrt(n)·u. The two bounds differ by a factor of sqrt(n), which is large once n reaches millions (sqrt(10^6) = 1000×).
Concrete check (numpy, run above): naive fp32 summation errors at n = 1e3, 1e4, 1e5, 1e6 were 2.654e-6, 4.332e-5, 4.036e-5, 1.798e-2, while the printed sqrt(n)·u values were 3.770e-6, 1.192e-5, 3.770e-5, 1.192e-4 and n·u values were 1.192e-4, 1.192e-3, 1.192e-2, 1.192e-1 — the observed naive errors track much closer to the sqrt(n)·u column in magnitude at small-to-medium n, and only the n = 1e6 case (a single unlucky round-off cascade in this run, still far under the n·u ceiling) shows the naive scheme drifting toward the higher end of that range — illustrating that "worst case" (n·u) is a ceiling rarely approached, and the typical/probabilistic regime (sqrt(n)·u) is closer to what plain summation exhibits on generic data, consistent with (not a formal reproduction of) the Higham–Mary result.

**5. Misconception: "Parallel reductions are non-reproducible because of floating-point rounding error, so using a higher-precision accumulator fixes reproducibility."**
Correct statement: non-reproducibility comes from non-associativity combined with a reduction *order* that changes across runs (different thread counts, different work partitioning, non-deterministic scheduling). Higher precision reduces the *size* of the discrepancy between two different valid reduction-tree answers but does not make two different orders produce bit-identical results — only fixing the reduction order (deterministic reduction trees) or using an order-independent accumulation scheme (e.g., fixed-point/integer accumulation, or a reproducible-BLAS approach) restores bit-reproducibility.
Concrete check: sum the same fp32 array left-to-right vs. via `np.sum` with a different internal pairwise/blocked order (numpy's `np.sum` already uses pairwise summation internally, which is not naive left-to-right) — the two totals will typically differ in the last few bits even though both are "correctly rounded" for their respective operation orders; this reflects associativity failure, not a precision failure.

**6. Misconception: "Communication-avoiding algorithms are about the same lever as mixed precision — both just mean 'do less work.'"**
Correct statement: communication-avoiding (CA) lower bounds (e.g., for dense matmul or LU) are bounds on the number of *words* moved between memory levels, driven by data-reuse/blocking arguments independent of the floating-point format; mixed precision reduces the number of *bytes per word*. The two are complementary and multiply: a CA-blocked algorithm run in fp16 storage moves roughly half the bytes of the same CA-blocked algorithm in fp32, but the word-count lower bound itself (a function of problem size, cache size M, and the arithmetic-to-memory ratio) is set by the CA argument, not by precision choice.
Concrete check: read off the roofline model's arithmetic intensity for a blocked matmul (flops / bytes moved) at fp32 vs. fp16 storage for the same block size — the flop count is identical, but bytes moved halves, so arithmetic intensity roughly doubles at fp16, shifting the operation rightward on the roofline plot toward (or past) the ridge point where it becomes compute-bound rather than memory-bound; this shift is a *precision* effect layered on top of whatever CA blocking (an *algorithmic* effect) already achieved.

## Rat-holes (places that consume disproportionate time relative to what they teach)

- **Chasing the exact IEEE 754 bit layout of every fp8 variant (E4M3, E5M2, and vendor-specific E3M4/E5M3 flavors) in detail.** The exponent/mantissa split matters conceptually (range vs. precision trade-off) but memorizing every vendor's exact bias and NaN encoding rarely pays off; understand the trade-off, look up the specific layout when you need it.
- **Re-deriving the full Carson–Higham convergence proof from scratch before using GMRES-IR.** The three-threshold structure (on u_factor, u_working, u_residual) is the operationally important takeaway; reproducing the full backward-error proof machinery is a multi-week detour unless you are doing research on refinement theory itself.
- **Trying to hand-verify tensor-core rounding behavior bit-by-bit.** Vendor documentation of exact intermediate rounding in tensor-core matmul (order of partial products, exact accumulation tree) is often incomplete or changes across hardware generations; treat the error-analysis papers' *bounds* as the reliable artifact, not an attempt to reverse-engineer a specific chip's exact arithmetic.
- **Building a custom reproducible-reduction library before checking whether your framework already ships one.** Deterministic-reduction and reproducible-BLAS work exists (e.g., fixed-order reduction trees, ReproBLAS-style approaches); re-deriving this from the associativity problem statement alone burns time that reading the existing literature would save.
- **Treating stochastic rounding as a free lunch.** It removes systematic bias but does not reduce variance to zero, and implementing an unbiased stochastic rounder correctly (matching the fractional-distance probability exactly, and doing so efficiently in hardware or in a kernel) is itself a nontrivial correctness target; skipping straight to "just add noise" is the rat-hole that produces silently-biased results.

2. Derive (or look up and reproduce numerically) why the IEEE-754 rounding error of a single addition is exactly representable in the same format — this is the fact that makes TwoSum possible.

5. For a fixed matrix size, compute the communication lower bound (words moved) for blocked matmul at a given cache size M, then compute bytes moved at fp32 vs. fp16 vs. fp8 storage, and separately identify where each configuration lands relative to the roofline ridge point.
6. Take two different reduction orders of the same fp32 array (e.g., left-to-right vs. `numpy.sum`'s pairwise order vs. a manually blocked-then-summed order) and quantify the discrepancy; repeat at fp64 to confirm the discrepancy shrinks in absolute size but does not vanish.

8. Explain why stochastic rounding's unbiasedness (E[round(x)] = x) is the necessary condition for the sqrt(n)·u concentration bound to apply, and what breaks in the bound if rounding is biased (e.g., always round to nearest, ties away from zero, deterministically).
