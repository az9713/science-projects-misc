Numerical Linear Algebra — Blind-Spot Pass Index (P6)

Twelve subtopics covering the field, graduate-student level. Each card lists the topic's scope and its three most consequential misconceptions, with a link to the full pass.

01

Floating point, conditioning, and backward stability

IEEE 754 formats, unit roundoff u = 2^-53, the standard model fl(x op y) = (x op y)(1+delta), condition numbers kappa(A), forward/backward error, and backward stability as the central standard for judging an algorithm.

Top misconceptions
  1. Binary64's ~15-17 decimal digits do not mean a computed answer is accurate to 15-17 digits — accuracy depends on kappa(A) times backward error, and can collapse to zero digits even with every operation correctly rounded.
  2. Catastrophic cancellation is not caused by subtraction being inaccurate; the subtraction itself is nearly exact. It amplifies pre-existing rounding error in the two inputs because the result's magnitude shrinks while the absolute error stays fixed.
  3. An ill-conditioned problem and an unstable algorithm are not the same idea: a stable algorithm can still return a bad answer on an ill-conditioned problem, and an unstable algorithm can look fine on a well-conditioned one.

02

Gaussian elimination, LU, and pivoting

LU via Gaussian elimination at (2/3)n^3 flops, partial/complete/rook pivoting, growth factor rho_n and Wilkinson's 2^(n-1) worst case, Cholesky and LDL^T, blocked/communication-avoiding variants, and cheap condition-number estimation.

Top misconceptions
  1. Partial pivoting is not "unstable" merely because rho_n can reach 2^(n-1) in Wilkinson's constructed worst case — that growth is vanishingly rare on random and most practical matrices.
  2. Pivoting does not make a solve accurate no matter what A is: pivoting controls the growth factor, but the solution's accuracy still depends on kappa(A).
  3. LU and Cholesky are not the same algorithm under different names — Cholesky exploits symmetric positive definiteness and needs no pivoting for stability, unlike general LU.

03

QR factorization and least squares

min ||Ax-b||_2, normal equations vs Householder/Givens QR, classical vs modified Gram-Schmidt loss of orthogonality (kappa(A)^2 u vs kappa(A) u), rank-revealing QRCP, and Tikhonov regularization.

Top misconceptions
  1. Normal equations and QR do not give the same accuracy — normal equations square the condition number (working with kappa(A)^2), while QR works with kappa(A) directly.
  2. Classical and modified Gram-Schmidt are not the same algorithm with harmless reordering: CGS loses orthogonality proportional to kappa(A)^2 u, MGS only to kappa(A) u.
  3. Reorthogonalizing more than twice does not keep improving accuracy — "twice is enough" is the actual rule, and further passes buy little beyond that.

04

The singular value decomposition

SVD A = U Sigma V^T, Eckart-Young-Mirsky optimal rank-k approximation, pseudoinverse, perturbation theory (Weyl, Wedin sin-theta), Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization, divide-and-conquer, and one-sided Jacobi SVD.

Top misconceptions
  1. Forming A^T A and eigendecomposing it is not a safe way to get the SVD — it squares the condition number and destroys accuracy for small singular values, unlike direct SVD algorithms.
  2. Truncating the SVD at rank k is optimal in both the Frobenius norm and the spectral (2-)norm simultaneously, by Eckart-Young-Mirsky, not just in Frobenius as sometimes assumed.
  3. The pseudoinverse A⁺ is not automatically well-behaved just because A has full rank — it can still be extremely sensitive to perturbation when A is near rank-deficient (small but nonzero smallest singular value).

05

Dense eigenvalue algorithms

Power/inverse/Rayleigh-quotient iteration, reduction to Hessenberg/tridiagonal form, QR algorithm with Wilkinson/Francis shifts, symmetric tridiagonal methods (MRRR), Bauer-Fike conditioning, pseudospectra, and the QZ algorithm for Ax = lambda Bx.

Top misconceptions
  1. The condition number of an eigenvalue is not the condition number of the matrix — each eigenvalue has its own condition number 1/|y*x|, and these can differ by orders of magnitude across eigenvalues of the same matrix.
  2. Rayleigh quotient iteration does not always converge, nor always at cubic rate — cubic convergence is local and holds for symmetric/normal matrices; general non-normal matrices get only quadratic convergence, and RQI is not globally convergent.
  3. Reduction to Hessenberg/tridiagonal form is not itself an approximation like the later QR steps — it is an exact similarity transform (up to unit roundoff) in finite O(n^3) operations, with no iteration or tolerance involved.

06

Krylov subspace methods

Krylov subspaces K_k(A,b), Arnoldi/Lanczos, CG with bound 2((sqrt(kappa)-1)/(sqrt(kappa)+1))^k, MINRES/SYMMLQ, GMRES(m), BiCGSTAB, finite-precision loss of orthogonality, and Krylov eigensolvers.

Top misconceptions
  1. The CG convergence bound 2((sqrt(kappa)-1)/(sqrt(kappa)+1))^k is a worst-case bound over all right-hand sides and eigenvalue distributions, not a prediction of the actual iteration count for a specific problem.
  2. Lanczos vectors do not stay orthogonal just because the three-term recurrence guarantees it in exact arithmetic — finite precision causes measurable loss of orthogonality that the recurrence alone does not prevent.
  3. GMRES(m) restarted is not simply "GMRES done in cheaper chunks" that converges to the same answer more slowly — restarting can stagnate or fail to converge at all where full GMRES would succeed.

07

Preconditioning

Left/right/split preconditioning, Jacobi/Gauss-Seidel/SOR as preconditioners, ILU(0)/ILU(k)/ILUT, sparse approximate inverses, algebraic/geometric multigrid, domain decomposition (Schwarz, FETI, BDDC), and mesh-independent convergence.

Top misconceptions
  1. A diagonal (Jacobi) preconditioner does not "fix" ill-conditioning in general — it rescales the matrix and can help when conditioning stems from scaling differences, but it does not address ill-conditioning from other sources.
  2. GMRES's reported residual norm does not always mean the true residual ||b - Ax||; with certain preconditioning or restart choices it can be a preconditioned or otherwise transformed residual that diverges from the true one.
  3. ILU(0) does not always exist, and is not simply "a slower version of full LU" — it can break down (encounter a zero or unstable pivot) even when full LU with pivoting succeeds.

08

Sparse direct methods

CSR/CSC/COO storage, fill-in and elimination graphs/trees, fill-reducing orderings (AMD, nested dissection, RCM), supernodal/multifrontal factorization, and nested dissection complexity: O(n^1.5) flops in 2D vs O(n^2) in 3D.

Top misconceptions
  1. A sparse matrix does not stay roughly sparse after direct factorization — fill-in during elimination can turn a sparse A into a dense (or far denser) factor, which is exactly why fill-reducing orderings matter.
  2. Fill-reducing orderings like AMD or nested dissection do not find the exactly optimal ordering — they are heuristics that reduce fill substantially but do not guarantee the minimum possible fill-in.
  3. Nested dissection does not scale the same way in 3D as in 2D with just a different constant — 2D gives O(n^1.5) flops and O(n log n) fill, while 3D gives O(n^2) flops and O(n^(4/3)) fill, a genuine change in growth rate.

09

Randomized numerical linear algebra

Random sketching (Gaussian, SRHT, CountSketch), Johnson-Lindenstrauss, randomized range finder and randomized SVD with oversampling/power iteration (Halko-Martinsson-Tropp), Nystrom approximation, sketch-and-solve/precondition least squares, and randomized trace estimation.

Top misconceptions
  1. A random Gaussian sketch's distortion of vector norms does not depend on the ambient dimension d — by the JL lemma, distortion concentrates at a rate governed by the sketch size k alone; d drops out of the bound.
  2. Randomized SVD's approximation quality is not "about as good as" the exact SVD by default — its error depends on the singular value decay and the amount of oversampling/power iteration used, and can be far worse without enough of either.
  3. Sketch-and-solve for least squares does not give the same answer as solving the original problem — it gives a solution to the sketched problem, whose accuracy relative to the true least-squares solution depends on the embedding's distortion.

10

Structured and data-sparse matrices

Banded, Toeplitz, Hankel, circulant (FFT-diagonalizable, O(n log n)), Cauchy, Vandermonde matrices, displacement rank, Levinson-Durbin, Bartels-Stewart for Sylvester/Lyapunov equations, and hierarchical low-rank formats (H-matrices, HSS, HODLR).

Top misconceptions
  1. A circulant matrix's eigenvalues do not need a general eigenvalue solver — the FFT diagonalizes circulant matrices directly, computing the eigenvalues themselves in O(n log n), not just speeding up matrix-vector products.
  2. Levinson-Durbin is not simply a faster way to solve any Toeplitz system — it can be numerically unstable for certain (e.g. non-positive-definite or ill-conditioned) Toeplitz matrices where a general solver remains the safer choice.
  3. Bartels-Stewart is not an approximate or iterative method for the Sylvester equation — it is a direct method, based on Schur decomposition, that computes the exact solution (up to floating-point rounding) in a finite number of steps.

11

Mixed-precision and hardware-aware algorithms

fp16 (u=2^-11), bfloat16 (u=2^-8), fp8, TF32, mixed-precision iterative refinement (Carson-Higham GMRES-IR), probabilistic error bounds growing as sqrt(n)u rather than nu, stochastic rounding, Kahan/TwoSum compensated summation, and roofline-model communication-avoiding algorithms.

Top misconceptions
  1. fp16 and bf16 do not differ only in decimal digits of precision — they trade off precision against exponent range differently (bf16 keeps fp32's exponent range with fewer mantissa bits), which changes which failure mode (overflow vs. rounding) dominates.
  2. Mixed-precision iterative refinement does not always converge just because refinement runs at higher precision than the factorization — convergence depends on kappa(A) staying within specific bounds tied to the precisions used (Carson-Higham conditions).
  3. Kahan summation and other error-free transformations do not give you the equivalent of secretly switching to fp64 or fp128 — they reduce the accumulated rounding error of a specific operation (like summation) without changing the working precision elsewhere.
Read full pass →
12

Matrix functions and matrix equations

f(A) via Jordan form/Cauchy integral/polynomial interpolation, Frechet-derivative conditioning, matrix exponential by scaling and squaring with Pade approximants, Schur-Parlett, matrix square root/logarithm/sign via Newton iterations, and f(A)b via Krylov/rational Krylov methods.

Top misconceptions
  1. Computing f(A) = V f(D) V^-1 via diagonalization is not numerically fine for any diagonalizable A — when V is ill-conditioned (A is close to non-diagonalizable), this approach amplifies error badly, unlike Schur-Parlett or scaling-and-squaring.
  2. A Taylor series for exp(A) with 20 or 30 terms is not "surely plenty" — for A with large norm, the series converges too slowly and suffers catastrophic cancellation, which is exactly why scaling and squaring with Pade approximants is used instead.
  3. Newton's method for sign(A) or sqrtm does not always converge quadratically and safely — it can be numerically unstable or fail to converge for matrices with eigenvalues near the branch cut or near zero, requiring safeguards.
