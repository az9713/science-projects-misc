Lesson 7 — Forming Instead of Following: Droop Is a Swing Equation

Series: Power-system dynamics for programmers. Lesson 7 of 8. This lesson shows that a droop-controlled grid-forming inverter, once you write its power filter as a first-order lag, is algebraically the same swing equation as Lesson 1's synchronous machine — and then shows exactly where that equivalence breaks down: the equation matches, the energy behind it does not.

The opening problem

A battery inverter has no rotor. It has no spinning steel, no moment of inertia, nothing that resists a change of speed by Newton's law. A system operator, used to synchronous machines, asks it for inertia anyway. Can code give a rotor-less converter something that behaves like inertia, how much of it, and — if the converter provides it — what physically backs the response when the grid calls on it?

Answer, computed by this lesson, in two parts. First: a droop controller with a first-order power filter already obeys the swing equation, with an equivalent inertia constant Heq = 1 / (2 mp ωc) — 1.59 s for 5 % droop and a 1 Hz filter, 0.45 of the 3.5 s machine used in Lesson 2. Second: that number describes a control law, not an energy store. The energy sits in the inverter's DC capacitor, and at the example ratings used below (20 mF, 1200 V, 1 MVA) that capacitor holds 14.4 kJ per MVA — 1/243 of the 3.5 MJ per MVA a 3.5 s machine holds. The control-law answer to "how much inertia" and the energy-store answer to "how much energy is actually there to give" are two different numbers, and a vendor's inertia claim means nothing until both are on the table together with the current limit that ultimately caps the response.

The stakes are not academic. Grids with a shrinking fleet of synchronous machines have less kinetic energy spinning at every moment, which by Lesson 1's result means a faster rate of change of frequency after any given loss of power. Converter-based generation is being asked to make up the difference. Whether it can depends on separating two questions that a single word — "inertia" — tends to blur together: does the controller's equation look like a swing equation, and does the energy behind the controller look like a rotor's kinetic energy. This lesson answers the first question with an exact derivation and the second with two capacitor calculations that are not close.

The model and its symbols

Grid-forming, defined by terminal behaviour

A converter is grid-forming when its controller makes it behave, at its AC terminals, like a voltage source E behind an impedance: it sets a voltage magnitude and an angle θ, and current flows into or out of the grid as a consequence of that voltage against whatever the grid's own voltage happens to be. The defining feature is that θ is an internal state: it is produced by integrating something inside the controller (a power imbalance, in this lesson), and it exists and evolves whether or not anything is currently measuring the grid's own angle.

A converter is grid-following when its controller instead measures the grid's voltage angle — typically with a phase-locked loop, the subject of Lesson 5 — and uses that measured angle, θmeas, as an input to command a current. Here θmeas is an output of a measurement, not a state the controller owns; if the grid voltage disappears, a grid-following converter has nothing to lock onto and no angle to command from.

Fig. 7.1. Grid-following (left) commands a current from a measured angle; grid-forming (right) commands a voltage from an angle it produces itself by integration. The network — a reactance X and the bus V∠0 — is the same in both panels. The source differs: a controlled current source I∠θmeas on the left, a controlled voltage source E∠θ on the right. Nothing about the wires or the reactance changes; what changes is whether θ is read from the grid or written to it by the controller's own state.

P-f droop with a first-order power filter

The grid-forming law this lesson analyses sets frequency as an affine function of measured power, through a filter that removes noise and switching ripple before the measurement reaches the droop gain:

ω  =  ωref − mp (Pf − Pref)
dPf/dt  =  ωc (P − Pf)

with symbols, all defined before use:

  • ω, ωref — the converter's commanded electrical frequency and its reference, in per unit of rated frequency (the derivation and the code use per unit throughout, so mp is in pu frequency per pu power).
  • mp — the droop gain: the frequency change per unit of sustained power deviation. 5 % droop, the value used throughout this lesson, means mp = 0.05, i.e. a full-scale power error moves frequency 5 % of rated.
  • P — the converter's instantaneous measured output power (p.u.); Pf — that same power after the first-order lag; Pref — the dispatch set-point.
  • ωc — the filter's corner (cutoff) angular frequency, rad/s. A "1 Hz filter" means ωc = 2π×1.

Without the filter, droop alone is a static map from power to frequency with no frequency dynamics (the angle θ is still the integral of ω, but frequency itself follows power instantly). The filter is what turns the frequency law into a differential equation with a state (Pf) and therefore something that can, in principle, look like a swing equation. The next section shows that it does, exactly.

The key derivation

Define the power imbalance dP = Pe − Pref, where Pe is the electrical output power, the same quantity as the filter input P. Write s for the time derivative d/dt. Let Δω (written dw) be the per-unit deviation of ω from ωref, so the droop law reads Δω = −mp (Pf − Pref). Differentiate it and substitute the filter equation for dPf/dt:

s Δω  =  −mp ωc (P − Pf)  =  −ωc Δω − mp ωc dP

(the second equality uses P − Pf = dP + (Pref − Pf) = dP + Δω/mp, because the droop law gives Pref − Pf = Δω/mp; then multiplies through by −mp ωc). Lesson 1's swing equation, at constant mechanical power Pm set equal to the dispatch Pref (so that Pm − Pe = −dP), is s Δω = −dP/(2H) − (KD/2H) Δω. The two equations have the same form — a first-order lag in Δω driven by dP — and matching coefficients term by term gives, derived here:

mp ωc = 1 / (2 H)   and   ωc = KD / (2 H)    ⇒    Heq = 1 / (2 mp ωc),   KD,eq = 1 / mp

This match is exact under exactly one assumption already built into the two equations above: the power filter is a genuine first-order lag and the droop law is linear over the range in play. Neither equation was linearised or approximated to reach this result — the two differential equations are identical, coefficient for coefficient, whenever that filter-and-droop structure holds; a higher-order filter or a saturating droop curve would break the exact match and leave only an approximation. Restoring a synchronizing coefficient

Lesson 2 showed that a synchronous machine against an infinite bus has a synchronizing coefficient Ks = (E V / X) cos(δ0), the slope of electrical power against angle at the operating point, and that Ks is what makes the linearised swing equation a stable, oscillatory second-order system rather than a runaway. A grid-forming converter, because it sets a voltage E∠θ behind a reactance X exactly as Fig. 7.1 draws it, restores the same Ks of the same form. Using Lesson 2's grid (E = 1.1, V = 1, X = 0.65, P = 0.8, 60 Hz) with the droop equivalent Heq = 1.592 s, KD,eq = 20 pu (the ωc = 2π×1 row of the table): Ks = 1.4913 pu/rad, natural frequency ωn = 13.29 rad/s (2.12 Hz), damping ratio ζ = 0.236, eigenvalues −3.142 ± j12.91. The real part, −3.142 1/s, equals exactly −ωc/2 — the filter sets the decay rate directly. The damping ratio is 14.8 times the 0.0159 that Lesson 2 computed for the synchronous machine at the same operating point: this particular droop-plus-filter combination oscillates far less than the rotor it is being compared against, because KD,eq = 1/mp = 20 pu is a large damping coefficient by machine standards.

Matching control: the energy is somewhere else

A second grid-forming design, matching control, ties the converter's frequency to its DC-link voltage (ω proportional to vdc, so the angle is the integral of that frequency) instead of to a droop-filtered power measurement, but the same bookkeeping question applies: how much energy backs the equivalent inertia it implies? The DC-link capacitor, of capacitance Cdc charged to vdc0, stores Edc = ½ Cdc vdc02, and the matching inertia constant on the converter's base Sbase is Heq = ½ Cdc vdc02 / Sbase. With Cdc = 20 mF, vdc0 = 1200 V, Sbase = 1 MVA (Example 3.2 of P1): Edc = 14.4 kJ, Heq = 0.0144 s. A 3.5 s machine on the same 1 MVA base holds 3.5 MJ; the ratio 3.5 / 0.0144 = 243. The capacitor is not a small rotor. It is a qualitatively different, much smaller energy store, per unit of rating, than any rotating machine on the grid today.

A separate way to see the same gap: an inertial response of Hv = 5 s on a 100 MVA plant, released over a 0.8 Hz frequency excursion at 50 Hz, delivers energy ≈ 2Hv S df / f0 = 16 MJ = 4.44 kWh (the first-order value; the exact kinetic-energy change Hv S [1 − (1 − df/f0)2] is 15.87 MJ) — 1111 times the 14.4 kJ the 1 MVA DC link above holds. That 1111 figure is real but it mixes ratings: 16 MJ is the demand from a 100 MVA plant, 14.4 kJ is the supply from a 1 MVA capacitor. Scaled to the same 1 MVA basis, the demand is 0.16 MJ, and the per-MVA ratio is 0.16 MJ / 14.4 kJ = 11.1 — still a real shortfall, but a factor of 11, not 1111. Even 11.1 is a lower bound, because it assumes the capacitor can be drained to 0 V. A converter must keep its DC voltage near vdc0 to keep modulating: if it tolerates a 10 % dip, the capacitor releases 1 − 0.92 = 19 % of 14.4 kJ, or 2.74 kJ, and the per-MVA ratio becomes 0.16 MJ / 2.74 kJ = 58. Both numbers are printed below; Fig. 7.3 shows why they answer different questions and neither should be quoted without saying which.

The code

The listing below runs all three checks. Part 1 integrates the filtered droop law and its matched swing equation side by side, with forward Euler at h = 10−4 s, under a constant 0.1 pu imbalance step, and prints the largest gap between the two trajectories at any step. Because the two equations were shown above to be algebraically identical — not merely similar — this is an identity check of the algebra on one linear ODE, not an independent physics test: any nonzero gap would mean an arithmetic slip in the coefficient matching, not new information about droop control. Part 2 puts the actual nonlinear droop-and-filter converter (states θ and Pf, power Pe = (E V / X) sin(θ)) against the Lesson 2 grid, steps Pref from 0.8 to 0.85, integrates with RK4, and measures the oscillation frequency and decay rate from the peaks of the transient — this time a genuine check, since the nonlinear sine has not been linearised anywhere in the simulated model. Part 3 prints the two energy ratios of Fig. 7.3.

"""Droop-as-swing checks: algebra identity, RK4 vs eigenvalues, energy.

Swing convention: 2H d(dw)/dt = P_m - P_e - K_D dw
                   d(delta)/dt = omega_0 dw
Stdlib only. Run: python lesson7_droop_vs_swing.py
"""
import math

M_P = 0.05
OMEGA_C = 2.0 * math.pi * 1.0
H_EQ = 1.0 / (2.0 * M_P * OMEGA_C)
K_D_EQ = 1.0 / M_P
DP_STEP = 0.1
H_STEP = 1e-4
N_STEPS = 200000

# Part 1: filtered droop law vs its matched swing equation, same step.
dw_droop = 0.0
dw_swing = 0.0
max_diff = 0.0
for _ in range(N_STEPS):
    ddw_droop = -OMEGA_C * dw_droop - M_P * OMEGA_C * DP_STEP
    ddw_swing = (-DP_STEP - K_D_EQ * dw_swing) / (2.0 * H_EQ)
    dw_droop += H_STEP * ddw_droop
    dw_swing += H_STEP * ddw_swing
    diff = abs(dw_droop - dw_swing)
    if diff > max_diff:
        max_diff = diff
print("Part 1: identity check of the algebra, not a physics test")
print("H_eq = %.3f s, K_D_eq = %.1f pu" % (H_EQ, K_D_EQ))
print("largest |dw_droop - dw_swing| = %.1e pu" % max_diff)
print("dw_droop final = %.6f pu (-m_p * dP = %.6f)"
      % (dw_droop, -M_P * DP_STEP))

# Part 2: nonlinear droop converter against the Lesson 2 grid.
E, V, X = 1.1, 1.0, 0.65
OMEGA_0 = 2.0 * math.pi * 60.0
P_MAX = E * V / X
P_REF0, P_REF1 = 0.8, 0.85
THETA0 = math.asin(P_REF0 / P_MAX)
K_S = P_MAX * math.cos(THETA0)


def deriv(theta, p_f, p_ref):
    dw = -M_P * (p_f - p_ref)
    p_e = P_MAX * math.sin(theta)
    return OMEGA_0 * dw, OMEGA_C * (p_e - p_f)


def rk4(theta, p_f, p_ref, h):
    k1 = deriv(theta, p_f, p_ref)
    k2 = deriv(theta + 0.5 * h * k1[0], p_f + 0.5 * h * k1[1], p_ref)
    k3 = deriv(theta + 0.5 * h * k2[0], p_f + 0.5 * h * k2[1], p_ref)
    k4 = deriv(theta + h * k3[0], p_f + h * k3[1], p_ref)
    dtheta = (k1[0] + 2 * k2[0] + 2 * k3[0] + k4[0]) / 6.0
    dpf = (k1[1] + 2 * k2[1] + 2 * k3[1] + k4[1]) / 6.0
    return theta + h * dtheta, p_f + h * dpf


theta, p_f, t, h = THETA0, P_REF0, 0.0, 1e-4
theta_eq1 = math.asin(P_REF1 / P_MAX)
peaks = []
prev, prev2 = theta, theta
for i in range(300000):
    theta, p_f = rk4(theta, p_f, P_REF1, h)
    t += h
    err = theta - theta_eq1
    err_prev = prev - theta_eq1
    err_prev2 = prev2 - theta_eq1
    if err_prev > err and err_prev > err_prev2:
        peaks.append((t - h, err_prev))
    prev2, prev = prev, theta
sigma_meas = math.log(abs(peaks[0][1] / peaks[1][1])) / (peaks[1][0]
                                                           - peaks[0][0])
omega_d_meas = 2.0 * math.pi / (peaks[1][0] - peaks[0][0])
print("\nPart 2: RK4 on the nonlinear droop converter vs the L2 grid")
print("K_s = %.4f pu/rad" % K_S)
print("measured omega_d = %.2f rad/s (target 12.91)" % omega_d_meas)
print("measured sigma = %.3f 1/s (target -3.142)" % -sigma_meas)

# Part 3: stored-energy ratios.
C_DC, V_DC0, S_BASE = 0.020, 1200.0, 1.0e6
E_DC = 0.5 * C_DC * V_DC0 ** 2
H_MATCH = E_DC / S_BASE
H_MACHINE = 3.5
RATIO_MACHINE_DC = H_MACHINE / H_MATCH
E_INERTIAL_100MVA = 16.0e6
RATIO_MISMATCHED = E_INERTIAL_100MVA / E_DC
RATIO_PER_MVA = (E_INERTIAL_100MVA / 100.0) / E_DC
print("\nPart 3: stored energy ratios")
print("E_dc = %.1f kJ, H_eq(matching) = %.4f s" % (E_DC / 1e3, H_MATCH))
print("machine 3.5 s / DC link: ratio = %.0f" % RATIO_MACHINE_DC)
print("16 MJ (100 MVA) / 14.4 kJ (1 MVA), mismatched ratings: %.0f"
      % RATIO_MISMATCHED)
print("per-MVA: 0.16 MJ / 14.4 kJ = %.1f" % RATIO_PER_MVA)

computed by the listing. Part 1's gap of 8.7×10−19 pu is floating-point roundoff, not model error — the two update rules are the same real number computed two different ways. Relative to the 0.005 pu signal the gap is about 2×10−16, the size of one rounding step in double precision, so no physical approximation is involved. The final value, −0.005 pu, equals −mp × 0.1 exactly, confirming the steady-state droop response independent of the filter speed. Part 2's measured values, 12.77 rad/s against a target of 12.91 (1.1 % low) and −3.148 1/s against −3.142 (0.2 % off), come from a simulation that never linearised anything. The decay rate does not depend on Ks (it is −ωc/2), so it matches closely. The 1.1 % frequency gap has a specific cause: the 12.91 target is linearised at the pre-step point (Pref = 0.8), but after the step the converter oscillates about the new equilibrium (Pref = 0.85), where Ks = 1.4634 pu/rad, ωn = 13.16 rad/s and ωd = 12.78 rad/s. The measured 12.77 is 0.1 % from that value. Linearise about the point the system settles to; a larger step moves that point further and widens the gap to the pre-step prediction.

What this model captures, and what it misses

  • Linear droop, one operating point. The coefficient match Heq, KD,eq needs no linearisation: it holds while the droop law is linear and the filter is first order. A droop curve that saturates breaks it. The synchronizing coefficient Ks and the eigenvalues are different: they hold only for small deviations around the operating angle, and they change when the operating point moves (Part 2 shows a 1.1 % frequency shift for a 0.05 pu step).
  • Current limit Imax ignored. Every result above assumes the converter can source or sink whatever current the voltage-behind-reactance model demands. A converter current-limited by its semiconductors is, the instant it saturates, no longer behaving as a voltage source at all — it has silently become a current source, and the entire grid-forming analysis of this lesson stops applying at that moment.
  • Capacitor alone, no source behind it. The capacitor energy calculation counts only the capacitor. Drained completely, 14.4 kJ supplies an extra 0.1 pu (100 kW on 1 MVA) for 0.144 s. In a battery inverter the battery refills the DC link through its converter, and the battery's much larger energy sustains any response longer than that: the capacitor supplies the first fraction of a second, the battery supplies everything after it.
  • Two other grid-forming designs named, not derived here. The virtual synchronous machine writes the swing equation directly, with chosen Hv, KD,v, rather than arriving at an equivalent one from droop and a filter (Exercise 2 compares their steady-state droop). Dispatchable virtual oscillator control (DVOC) instead couples converters through a nonlinear oscillator whose global synchronization result is cited [S11 — verify], not derived in this lesson: Colombino, Groß, Brouillon and Dörfler (2019), IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 64(11), 4496–4511.

Exercises

1. (Derivational) Derive Heq and KD,eq by coefficient matching between the filtered droop law and the swing equation.

Answer target

As in the key derivation above: Heq = 1 / (2 mp ωc), KD,eq = 1 / mp. The match is exact under the assumption that makes it exact: the power filter is a genuine first-order lag and the droop law is linear over the operating range — no other approximation enters.

2. (Derivational) Show that a virtual synchronous machine with Hv, KD,v has the same steady-state droop as a droop controller with mp = 1 / KD,v.

Answer target

Set derivatives to zero. The VSM's swing equation 2Hv dΔω/dt = −dP − KD,v Δω, at steady state (dΔω/dt = 0), gives Δωss = −dP / KD,v. Droop's steady state is Δωss = −mp dP. The two agree for every dP exactly when mp = 1 / KD,v, independent of Hv, which affects only the transient, not the steady state.

3. (Computational) Find the DC capacitance that gives Heq = 3.5 s at 1200 V on 1 MVA.

Answer target

Cdc = 2 Heq Sbase / vdc02 = 2 × 3.5 × 106 / 12002 = 4.861 F — 243 times the 20 mF used above, matching the ratio computed in the lesson.

4. (Conceptual) Why can a converter be grid-forming and still be unable to give an inertial response?

Answer target

Grid-forming is a statement about control structure: the converter holds an internal angle θ and presents a voltage source at its terminals, as Fig. 7.1 shows. Nothing in that structural definition guarantees an energy reserve to draw on, or headroom below the current limit Imax to deliver the extra current an inertial response demands. A grid-forming converter dispatched at its full current rating, or backed by a DC link with only the 14.4 kJ per MVA computed above and no battery behind it, forms the grid's voltage perfectly and still has nothing left to give when asked for inertia.

5. (Conceptual) A vendor says "5 seconds of inertia." Name the three numbers you need before the claim means anything.

Answer target

(1) The rating Sbase the 5 s is stated on — per Fig. 7.3, an Heq quoted per-unit of one rating and compared against an energy demand at a different rating produces the mismatched 1111, not the same-rating 11.1 (or 58 once only the usable energy above a 10 % DC-voltage dip is counted). (2) The usable energy reserve actually behind the control law — the DC capacitor alone, per this lesson, or the battery, storage plant, or governor behind it. (3) The current limit Imax and the headroom kept below it, since a saturated converter stops being a voltage source (Exercise 4) no matter what its control law claims.
