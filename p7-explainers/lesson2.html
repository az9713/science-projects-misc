One machine on a stiff grid: linearise, then read the eigenvalues

Lesson 2 · classical single-machine-infinite-bus (SMIB) model · H = 3.5 s, KD = 2 pu (Example 1.1)

Opening problem. A 60 Hz generator gets a small bump in mechanical power. It settles into an oscillation at about 1.4 Hz, and that oscillation takes tens of seconds to die out. Why that particular frequency, and why so slow to decay? This lesson answers both questions with two numbers computed from Example 1.1 of the P1 book: the natural frequency ωn = 8.96 rad/s = 1.43 Hz and the damping ratio ζ = 0.0159.

1. The model

Treat the generator as a constant-magnitude internal voltage E′ behind a fixed reactance X, connected through that reactance to an infinite bus of fixed voltage Vinf and fixed frequency. “Infinite bus” means the rest of the grid is so much larger than this one machine that nothing the machine does moves the bus voltage or frequency — a standard idealisation, not a claim about the size of any real grid. The angle between E′ and Vinf is the rotor angle δ, in electrical radians. Standard circuit analysis of this two-source, one-reactance network gives the real power the machine delivers:

Pe = (E′ Vinf / X) · sin(δ) = Pmax sin(δ)

with Pmax = E′ Vinf / X pu. This is the power-angle relation: a pure sine because the network here is a single lossless reactance. Every number below traces back to this one equation.

Convention used throughout: the swing equation is written 2H d(Δω)/dt = Pm − Pe − KDΔω, and dδ/dt = ω0Δω. Damping is always written KD, never D, to avoid confusion with any other D in a larger model.

This is the classical generator model: a constant flux behind fixed reactance, no excitation control, no saliency. Example 1.1 gives E′ = 1.1 pu directly, so this lesson needs no dq reference frame and no Park transformation.

2. Equilibrium: numerical and closed-form

The state is x = (δ, Δω). The ODEs are:

dδ/dt = ω0 Δω
2H d(Δω)/dt = Pm − Pmax sin(δ) − KDΔω

At equilibrium both derivatives are zero. The first equation, dδ/dt = ω0Δω = 0, forces Δω = 0: the rotor turns at exactly synchronous speed. Substituting Δω = 0 into the second equation leaves the torque balance:

Pm = Pmax sin(δ0) ⇒ δ0 = arcsin(Pm / Pmax)

That closed form is exact for this model; no iteration is needed because the network is a single sine nonlinearity. With the Example 1.1 numbers: Pmax = 1.1 × 1.0 / 0.65 = 1.6923 pu, Pm/Pmax = 0.4727, and

δ0 = arcsin(0.4727) = 0.4924 rad = 28.2115°

Note on the two versions of this number: the P1 book plan prints δ0 = 0.4926 rad, cos(δ0) = 0.8814 and Ks = 1.4915. Those values do not reproduce: the exact ratio 0.8/1.6923 gives δ0 = 0.4924 rad, cos(δ0) = 0.8812 and Ks = 1.4913, and even the rounded ratio 0.4727 gives 0.4925 rad, not 0.4926. This lesson uses the computed values from the Python script in Section 5, to four digits, and uses the three-digit values 1.49 pu/rad, 8.96 rad/s and 0.0159 only where it quotes the P1 book.

3. Linearising by hand: the synchronizing torque coefficient

The only nonlinear term in the swing equations is sin(δ). Near δ0, write δ = δ0 + Δδ for small Δδ, and use the first-order Taylor expansion sin(δ0 + Δδ) ≈ sin(δ0) + cos(δ0)·Δδ. Substituting into the swing equation and cancelling the equilibrium terms (Pm − Pmaxsin(δ0) = 0) leaves a linear equation in the deviations:

d(Δδ)/dt = ω0Δω,   2H d(Δω)/dt = −KsΔδ − KDΔω,   Ks = Pmax cos(δ0)

(Δω needs no second Δ: its equilibrium value is already 0, so it is its own deviation.)

Ks is the synchronizing torque coefficient: the slope of the power-angle curve at the operating point, in pu torque per radian. It plays the role of a spring constant — a positive Ks pulls the rotor angle back toward δ0 when disturbed, exactly like a mechanical spring pulling a mass back to rest. KD plays the role of a dashpot: it opposes the speed deviation Δω, which is the rate of change of δ, and so dissipates the oscillation’s energy over time. Numerically, Ks = 1.6923 × cos(28.2115°) = 1.6923 × 0.8812 = 1.4913 pu/rad.

In state-space form, with x = (Δδ, Δω), the linearised system is dx/dt = Ax with the Jacobian

A = [ [0, ω0], [−Ks/(2H), −KD/(2H)] ]

Section 5 checks this hand-derived A against a finite-difference Jacobian computed straight from the nonlinear f(x) with no algebra at all, as a way to catch a sign or factor-of-two error before trusting the eigenvalues.

4. Eigenvalues, ωn, and ζ

The characteristic polynomial of A is found from det(A − sI) = 0:

s² + (KD/2H)s + Ksω0/(2H) = 0

Matching this to the standard second-order form s² + 2ζωns + ωn² = 0 gives:

ωn = √(Ksω0 / 2H)    ζ = KD / (4Hωn)

This is why the oscillation sits near 1.4 Hz and not, say, 10 Hz or 0.1 Hz: ωn depends on the ratio of electrical stiffness (Ksω0) to mechanical inertia (2H). For a typical H of 2–8 s and Ks of 1–2 pu/rad, the formula gives 0.77 Hz (Ks = 1, H = 8) to 2.19 Hz (Ks = 2, H = 2). That range almost coincides with the 0.7–2 Hz band that power engineers call the local electromechanical mode [S01, Kundur 1994, Power System Stability and Control: typical H, §3.9, Table 3.2; local-mode band, §12.1 — verify both]. And it is slow to decay because ζ is small: with KD = 2 pu of damping torque against a stiffness-to-inertia ratio that gives ωn ≈ 9 rad/s, ζ comes out under 2%, far short of the 0.5–0.7 that would make the oscillation die out in a couple of cycles.

Full numbers from Example 1.1 (probe values, four digits): ωn = 8.9618 rad/s = 1.4263 Hz, ζ = 0.01594, and the eigenvalue pair is s = −0.1429 ± j8.9607 s−1. The real part, −0.1429 s−1, is exactly −ζωn = −KD/(4H); it sets the decay-envelope time constant at 1/0.1429 ≈ 7 s. The amplitude falls to e−4 ≈ 2% of its start value only after 4 time constants, about 28 s, which is why the disturbance takes tens of seconds, not seconds, to die away. The imaginary part, 8.9607 rad/s, is the damped natural frequency ωn√(1−ζ²), only 0.001 rad/s below the undamped ωn because ζ is so small.

5. Code: derive it, then check it

The listing below is a condensed excerpt (75 non-blank lines, no print statements) of lesson2_smib.py, standard library only. It does four things: (a) defines the nonlinear state derivative f(x); (b) builds a finite-difference Jacobian with step 1e-6 to compare with the hand-derived analytic A (the full script prints both); (c) gets the eigenvalues from the quadratic formula with cmath.sqrt so complex results are not a special case; and (d) runs a fixed-step RK4 integration at h = 0.001 s for 10 s after stepping Pm from 0.8 to 0.85 pu, then measures the oscillation frequency from zero crossings of δ − δnew. Every number quoted anywhere in this lesson that is not simple arithmetic comes from an actual run of this script, not from a hand estimate. 

"""SMIB classical model: equilibrium, linearisation, eigenvalues, RK4.

Swing convention: 2H d(dw)/dt = P_m - P_e - K_D dw
                   d(delta)/dt = omega_0 dw
Stdlib only. Run: python lesson2_smib.py
"""
import cmath
import math

H = 3.5
K_D = 2.0
E_P = 1.1
V_INF = 1.0
X = 0.65
OMEGA_0 = 2.0 * math.pi * 60.0
P_MAX = E_P * V_INF / X


def f(x, p_m):
    """State derivative. x = (delta, dw)."""
    delta, dw = x
    ddelta = OMEGA_0 * dw
    ddw = (p_m - P_MAX * math.sin(delta) - K_D * dw) / (2.0 * H)
    return (ddelta, ddw)


def analytic_jacobian(delta0):
    k_s = P_MAX * math.cos(delta0)
    return ((0.0, OMEGA_0),
            (-k_s / (2.0 * H), -K_D / (2.0 * H)))


def fd_jacobian(x0, p_m, step=1e-6):
    n = len(x0)
    jac = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    f0 = f(x0, p_m)
    for j in range(n):
        xp = list(x0)
        xp[j] += step
        fp = f(tuple(xp), p_m)
        for i in range(n):
            jac[i][j] = (fp[i] - f0[i]) / step
    return jac


def eigenvalues_2x2(a):
    tr = a[0][0] + a[1][1]
    det = a[0][0] * a[1][1] - a[0][1] * a[1][0]
    disc = cmath.sqrt(tr * tr - 4.0 * det)
    return ((tr + disc) / 2.0, (tr - disc) / 2.0)


def rk4_step(x, p_m, h):
    k1 = f(x, p_m)
    x2 = (x[0] + 0.5 * h * k1[0], x[1] + 0.5 * h * k1[1])
    k2 = f(x2, p_m)
    x3 = (x[0] + 0.5 * h * k2[0], x[1] + 0.5 * h * k2[1])
    k3 = f(x3, p_m)
    x4 = (x[0] + h * k3[0], x[1] + h * k3[1])
    k4 = f(x4, p_m)
    dd = (k1[0] + 2 * k2[0] + 2 * k3[0] + k4[0]) / 6.0
    dw = (k1[1] + 2 * k2[1] + 2 * k3[1] + k4[1]) / 6.0
    return (x[0] + h * dd, x[1] + h * dw)

# --- equilibrium, Jacobian check, eigenvalues (base P_m = 0.8) ---
p_m0 = 0.8
delta0 = math.asin(p_m0 / P_MAX)
k_s = P_MAX * math.cos(delta0)
omega_n = math.sqrt(k_s * OMEGA_0 / (2.0 * H))
zeta = K_D / (4.0 * H * omega_n)
a_analytic = analytic_jacobian(delta0)
a_fd = fd_jacobian((delta0, 0.0), p_m0)
eig1, eig2 = eigenvalues_2x2(a_analytic)

# --- step P_m: 0.8 -> 0.85, RK4 h=0.001 s, 10 s, zero-crossing freq ---
p_m_new = 0.85
delta_new = math.asin(p_m_new / P_MAX)
h, n_steps = 0.001, 10000
x, t, prev_g, crossings = (delta0, 0.0), 0.0, delta0 - delta_new, []
for _ in range(n_steps):
    x = rk4_step(x, p_m_new, h)
    t += h
    g = x[0] - delta_new
    if prev_g < 0.0 <= g or prev_g > 0.0 >= g:
        crossings.append(t - h * g / (g - prev_g))
    prev_g = g
periods = [crossings[i + 2] - crossings[i]
           for i in range(len(crossings) - 2)]
freq_zc = 1.0 / (sum(periods) / len(periods))

Trap to state explicitly. The step goes from Pm = 0.8 to 0.85 pu, but the simulated oscillation runs at 1.4127 Hz (from zero crossings) or 1.4129 Hz (from the linearised formula at the new point), not the 1.4263 Hz derived at the start of this lesson. The reason is not a bug: the machine now oscillates about the new equilibrium, δnew = 30.1505°, where Ks = 1.4634 pu/rad (down from 1.4913, because the power-angle curve is flatter further from δ = 0) and so ωn = 8.8775 rad/s = 1.4129 Hz. Linearising at the new operating point matches the simulation (1.4129 Hz against the 1.4127 Hz zero-crossing estimate, a difference of 0.0002 Hz, about 0.01%). Most of that small difference has a known cause: zero crossings measure the damped frequency ωn√(1−ζ²), which at the new point is 8.8775 × √(1 − 0.01609²) / 2π = 1.41272 Hz, while 1.4129 Hz is the undamped ωn. The two numbers, 1.4263 Hz and 1.4127–1.4129 Hz, are both correct: they are the small-signal frequency at two different operating points.

6. Model limits

This is the classical model: it has no flux decay (E′ is held constant, so any excitation-system dynamics are invisible), no automatic voltage regulator or excitation control loop, no saliency (the reactance is the same on the d-axis and the q-axis, so there is no reluctance-torque term), and no stator resistance, so every watt of Pm in excess of Pe goes into either accelerating the rotor or into the damping term — none is lost to I²R in the stator windings [S01, Kundur 1994, §3.9 — verify]. The infinite bus is an idealisation on the other side of the reactance: fixed voltage magnitude and fixed frequency regardless of what this one machine does. None of these five omissions changes the shape of the answer this lesson gives — a spring-and-dashpot second-order system — but each one would add terms to Ks and KD in a fuller model. The classical model is the smallest model that still has a swing mode. Remove H and the system is first order, so it cannot oscillate. Set E′ to zero and there is no synchronizing power (Ks = 0). Let X go to zero and Ks becomes infinite, so the angle cannot move. The sine nonlinearity is not needed for the swing mode: a linear spring Pe = KsΔδ gives the same small-signal oscillation; the sine matters only for large swings and for the second, unstable equilibrium.

7. Exercises

1. Conceptual

A machine sits at δ0 = 95°. Is it small-signal stable?

Answer target

No. Ks = Pmaxcos(95°) = 1.6923×(−0.0872) = −0.1475 pu/rad, which is negative. With Ks < 0 the constant term of the characteristic polynomial, Ksω0/2H, is negative, so the product of the two roots is negative: one eigenvalue is real and positive, the other real and negative. A single positive real eigenvalue means the linearised system diverges monotonically, with no oscillation at all — the classic loss-of-synchronism instability on the falling half of the power-angle curve.

2. Derivational

Show that writing the damping term as KD′(ω − ω0) with ω in rad/s, while still keeping 2H d(Δω)/dt on the left with Δω in pu, multiplies the computed ζ by ω0.

Answer target

ω − ω0 in rad/s equals ω0·Δω in pu (since Δω is defined as the per-unit speed deviation). Substituting KD′·ω0Δω for what was KDΔω is equivalent to silently replacing KD with KD′ω0 everywhere it appears, including in ζ = KD/(4Hωn). So ζ becomes 376.99 × 0.01594 = 6.01, which is badly overdamped and wrong — a unit-mixing error, not a physical result. This is exactly why the house convention in this lesson fixes KD as a pu-to-pu coefficient and never lets an omega in rad/s multiply it a second time.

3. Computational

Repeat the eigenvalue computation for H = 2, 3.5, 6, 8 s.

Answer target

ωn = 11.86, 8.96, 6.84, 5.93 rad/s and ζ = 0.0211, 0.0159, 0.0122, 0.0105 for H = 2, 3.5, 6, 8 s respectively (script output, Section 5). Both quantities scale as H−0.5: ωn ∝ H−0.5 directly from its defining formula, and ζ = KD/(4Hωn) ∝ H−1·H0.5 = H−0.5 since KD is held fixed. A heavier rotor (larger H) both oscillates more slowly and damps out more slowly, for the same electrical stiffness and the same damping torque.

4. Computational

Replace the analytic Jacobian by the finite-difference one for step sizes 1e-2 down to 1e-12, and report where the error is smallest.

Answer target

The run in this lesson’s script reports the smallest error at step = 1e-08, with |A[1][0]analytic − A[1][0]fd| = 8.234e-10. The error falls monotonically from 5.750e-04 at step = 1e-02 down to that minimum, then rises again at step = 1e-09 (1.980e-08), 1e-10 (9.910e-08), 1e-11 (3.767e-07), and 1e-12 (3.549e-06). Two error terms explain the shape. The truncation error of a forward difference is (step/2)·∂²f/∂δ² = (step/2)·Pm/(2H) = 0.0571·step, which predicts 5.71e-04 at step = 1e-02 (observed 5.750e-04) and falls tenfold per decade, as observed. The round-off error comes from the terms of size about 0.8 inside f, each rounded to about ε = 2.2e-16 relative (IEEE double); after division by 2H and by the step it is about ε·0.8/(2H)/step ≈ 2.5e-17/step, that is 2.5e-09 at step = 1e-08 (observed 8.2e-10, the same order). The sum 0.0571·step + 2.5e-17/step is smallest at step = √(2.5e-17/0.0571) ≈ 2e-08, which matches the observed best step of 1e-08.

5. Conceptual

Say in one sentence why ζ = 0.016 motivates power system stabilisers.

Answer target

A damping ratio of 0.016 means the swing amplitude falls by only 9.5% per cycle (ratio exp(−2πζ/√(1−ζ²)) = 0.905), so a plant trip or a load step rings for tens of seconds; a power system stabiliser adds a supplementary signal to the excitation system that raises the effective damping torque (raises KD) and, ideally, leaves the synchronizing torque Ks unchanged, so the oscillation dies out faster while ωn stays the same (the damped frequency drops only slightly: 8.9607 rad/s at KD = 2 against 8.7018 rad/s at KD = 30, run output) [S01, Kundur 1994, §12.5 — verify].
