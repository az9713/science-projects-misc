Lesson 8 — From One Machine to a System: Centre of Inertia, Nadir, and Sizing

Series: Power-system dynamics for programmers. Lesson 8 of 8. Lessons 1 through 7 treated frequency as the output of one integrator, driven by one machine or one machine against an infinite bus. This lesson removes that simplification. A real grid is many machines coupled through a network, and it is answering three questions at once: how do several rotors add up to one shared frequency, how does that frequency dip after a real loss of infeed, and how much grid-forming plant does it take to replace a retiring fleet of spinning machines. All three are answered from the same two building blocks: a coupled swing equation and a linear-ramp energy balance.

The opening problem

On 9 August 2019, Great Britain's grid frequency fell to 48.8 Hz. Low-frequency demand disconnection — automatic, relay-triggered shedding of customer load — acted and shed about 931 MW, following a total loss of infeed of about 1878 MW cited [S14 — verify]. That 48.8 Hz line is not a round number chosen for teaching; it is the threshold at which Great Britain's own protection scheme starts shedding customers.

Take a smaller, cleaner version of the same event for a 50 Hz grid: a 1000 MW loss of infeed, met by 1000 MW of primary response delivered as a straight ramp over 10 s (so the imbalance is fully repaid at t = 10 s, not before). How much kinetic energy must be spinning on the grid at the instant of the loss to keep frequency above 48.8 Hz throughout the event?

Answer, computed by this lesson: at least 104.2 GVA·s. At 100 GVA·s — just under the floor — the nadir is 48.75 Hz, on the wrong side of the line. The rest of the lesson builds the equation that produces that floor, and two programs that check it.

The GB 2019 event needed a network of many machines, a governor and primary-response chain, and a load-shedding relay ladder. This lesson does not reconstruct that network; it builds the smallest model that still contains the two ideas the event turned on: many rotors act, to first order, like one shared frequency (the centre of inertia), and the size of a dip after a loss is set by one ratio — energy stored against power missing and for how long. Those same two ideas size the grid-forming converter capacity that replaces a retiring machine, the lesson's second half.

The model: many machines, one shared frequency

Lesson 1 wrote Newton's law for a single spinning rotor and divided by its own rating to get the per-unit swing equation. A network of several machines gives each machine its own equation of that same form, coupled to the others only through the electrical power it exchanges with the network. For machine i:

Mi ddwi/dt = Pm,i − Pe,i − KD,i dwi     dδi/dt = ω0 dwi

This is the same convention Lesson 1 fixed and every lesson since has kept: dwi is machine i's per-unit speed deviation (ωi − ω0)/ω0; δi is its rotor angle against a common synchronous reference, in radians; ω0 = 2π f0 is the base electrical angular frequency (314.16 rad/s at f0 = 50 Hz); Pm,i and Pe,i are mechanical input and electrical output power, in per unit on a common system base Ssys; and KD,i is a per-unit damping coefficient — always written KD in this series, never the bare letter D, to keep it distinct from a differential operator. Mi is machine i's inertia constant re-based to the system: Mi = 2 Hi Si / Ssys, where Hi is the machine's own inertia constant (seconds of stored kinetic energy per unit of its own rating Si).

Two machines exchange power through a tie that, linearized about an operating angle, behaves like a spring: Pe,1 = K12 (δ1 − δ2), with Pe,2 the same term, sign reversed, where K12 = ( E1 E2 / X12) cos(δ12,0) is the synchronizing coefficient (pu/rad) at the pre-disturbance angle δ12,0; E1, E2 are internal EMF magnitudes (pu, held constant here) and X12 is the tie reactance (pu) — the same linearization Lesson 1 used for one machine against an infinite bus, now applied between two finite machines.

The centre of inertia

If every machine's speed deviation stayed identical at every instant, the network would behave exactly like Lesson 1's single lumped rotor. It does not, in general — the machines swing against each other too — but there is still a well-defined "average" that a single-frequency instrument reads. The centre-of-inertia (COI) speed deviation is the inertia-weighted average of every machine's own deviation:

dwCOI = ( Σi Mi dwi ) / ( Σi Mi )

and the COI frequency is fCOI = f0(1 + dwCOI). Summing every machine's swing equation, the internal Pe,i tie-flow terms cancel (Machowski et al. 2008) cited [S05 — verify], leaving one aggregate equation in exactly Lesson 1's form, with Mi replaced by their sum and dwi by dwCOI. That aggregate equation is what the opening problem and the nadir formula below both use: a single-machine result still applies to a whole grid, provided the question is about the shared frequency and not about how the machines swing against one another.

The system's stored kinetic energy is the same sum, in energy units: Ekin,sys = Σi Hi Si, and Σi Mi = 2 Ekin,sys / Ssys.

Test system TS4

The two-machine example running through this lesson is test system TS4 of this series' companion textbook P1, 1000 MVA system base: unit 1 rated 400 MVA, H1 = 4.0 s; unit 2 rated 300 MVA, H2 = 3.0 s. System base: M1 = 2 × 4.0 × 400/1000 = 3.2 s, M2 = 2 × 3.0 × 300/1000 = 1.8 s. Each machine carries KD = 2 pu on its own rating, re-based to KD,1 = 2 × 400/1000 = 0.8 pu and KD,2 = 2 × 300/1000 = 0.6 pu. The coupling values — internal EMFs E1 = E2 = 1.05 pu, tie reactance X12 = 2.0 pu, transfer 0.2 pu — are not from TS4; they are assumed for this lesson to give the example a concrete, worked coupling.

The key derivation

The nadir formula and the factor 4

Take the aggregate (COI) equation from the model section, and add a loss of infeed of size dP (MW, a positive number), repaid by a straight-line primary-response ramp over duration T. The net power surplus on the grid is then −dP(1 − t/T) for 0 ≤ t ≤ T, and zero after T. Two substitutions turn the per-unit COI equation into energy and hertz: Σi Mi = 2 Ekin,sys / Ssys, and dwCOI = df/f0, where df = fCOI − f0 in Hz. Multiply both sides by Ssys to write power in MW:

(2 Ekin,sys / f0) d(df)/dt = −dP + dP t / T,    0 ≤ t ≤ T

With Ekin,sys in MVA·s (equal to MJ) and power in MW, both sides are in MW, and df, not dw, is the state. Load damping is set to zero here (see the model limits and Exercise 3). At t = 0 the right-hand side is −dP, giving RoCoF0 directly:

RoCoF0 = f0 dP / (2 Ekin,sys)

Integrating the right-hand side from 0 to t gives −dP t + dP t²/(2T). The ramp fully repays the loss at t = T, so the right-hand side is zero from T onward and df stops moving — the nadir is at exactly t = T. Evaluating there gives −dPT/2, so:

dfnadir = − f0 dP T / (4 Ekin,sys)

The factor 4 is two 2's from two integrations, not one: the first 2 sits in the coefficient 2Ekin,sys/f0 (the same 2 in every swing equation since Lesson 1); the second comes from integrating the ramp, ∫0T (1 − t/T) dt = T/2, which halves the naive product dP·T since the ramp tapers linearly to zero. Multiply both 2's into −f0dP/(2Ekin,sys) and the 4 falls out.

At Ekin,sys = 200 GVA·s assumed for this lesson (attach a year and a verify mark if you tie this to a real grid) — the value used in this series' Example 4.1 — and the opening problem's dP = 1000 MW, T = 10 s: RoCoF0 = 50×1000/(2×200000) = 0.125 Hz/s and fnadir = 49.375 Hz. Halving the stored energy to 100 GVA·s doubles both numbers: RoCoF0 = 0.25 Hz/s, fnadir = 48.75 Hz — below the GB event's 48.8 Hz line. At 300 GVA·s the nadir is 49.583 Hz; at 50 GVA·s it is 47.5 Hz. Solving the nadir formula for the smallest Ekin,sys that keeps a drop no larger than 1.2 Hz (50 − 48.8) gives the opening problem's floor:

Ekin,sys ≥ f0 dP T / (4 × 1.2) = 50 × 1000 × 10 / 4.8 = 104.2 GVA·s

Every curve reaches its nadir at exactly t = T = 10 s (marked dots) and is flat after, since the ramp fully repays the loss there. Doubling Ekin,sys from 100 to 200 GVA·s halves the dip depth, 1.25 Hz to 0.625 Hz — nadir depth scales as 1/Ekin,sys, exactly as RoCoF0 does. The 4-by-4 state matrix and its eigenvalues

The pre-disturbance tie angle follows from the transfer: P12 = (E1E2 / X12) sin(δ12,0), so 0.2 = (1.05×1.05/2.0) sin(δ12,0), giving δ12,0 = 21.27°. The linearized synchronizing coefficient at that angle is K12 = (1.05×1.05/2.0) cos(21.27°) = 0.5137 pu/rad. With state x = (δ1, δ2, dw1, dw2) the linearized system is ẋ = A x with

A = [ 0, 0, ω0, 0 ]
[ 0, 0, 0, ω0 ]
[ −K12/M1, K12/M1, −KD1/M1, 0 ]
[ K12/M2, −K12/M2, 0, −KD2/M2 ]

Its eigenvalues, computed in the listing below, are 0 1/s, −0.280 1/s, and −0.1517 ± j11.83 1/s. Three facts, not one, come out of this:

  • Zero is not an instability. δ1 and δ2 only ever appear in A as the difference δ1 − δ2: shifting both angles by the same constant changes no row and no power flow, since flow depends only on the angle difference across the tie, never on an absolute angle. There is no physical reference for "the" angle of the system, so a mode that only moves both angles together is expected, not a defect.
  • −0.280 1/s is the common (COI) mode: it matches −(KD1+KD2) /(M1+M2) = −(0.8+0.6)/(3.2+1.8) = −0.280 1/s. The solver gives −0.280003 1/s. The formula is exact only when KD1/M1 = KD2/M2; here the two ratios are 0.25 and 0.333 1/s, so it holds to five decimals, not exactly. System-wide speed decays at total damping over total inertia, the same ratio the aggregate equation above predicts on its own.
  • The complex pair is the relative mode: the two machines swinging against each other at 1.884 Hz (=11.83/2π), damping ratio 0.0128 — lightly damped, since KD is small here and the tie is stiff. Independent check: ωn = √( ω0 K12 (1/M1 + 1/M2)) = 11.8358 rad/s. This equals the eigenvalue's magnitude |λ| = 11.8358 rad/s. The imaginary part, 11.8348 rad/s, is the damped frequency ωn√(1 − ζ²), 0.001 rad/s lower. The listing prints 11.84 and 11.83 only because the two values round to different sides of 11.835. One network, two kinds of mode: the relative mode exists only because there is more than one rotor to swing against another.

Right: all three eigenvalues on one complex plane, real-axis scale (±0.5 over 260 px, 260 px per unit) about 20 times finer than the imaginary-axis scale (±14 over 360 px, 12.9 px per unit) — a single linear scale would make the two near-origin points indistinguishable. The relative mode moves them opposite directions, amplitude ∝ 1/Mi: unit 2 (M2 = 1.8 s) swings 1.78 times further than unit 1 (M1 = 3.2 s), since 1/1.8 ÷ 1/3.2 = 1.78.

Sizing a grid-forming fleet: three constraints, three units

A different question: not what a given amount of inertia does to a given loss, but how much grid-forming converter capacity must replace a retiring fleet of spinning machines. Take the sizing case from this series' Example 4.2: 8 synchronous condensers — a synchronous machine spun with no prime mover, giving inertia and reactive support only — each rated 200 MVA, 1.6 GVA total. (The project brief's own label is "1.6 GW"; a condenser is rated in MVA, so this lesson keeps the correct unit. The fleet is a design case set by this book's plan, not a published, in-service one.) Each condenser carries H = 3.5 s including its flywheel assumed for this lesson (cite a manufacturer figure with verify if one is used), so Ekin = 8 × 200 × 3.5 = 5600 MVA·s = 5.6 GVA·s. Size a replacement against three constraints, in three separate units, never added:

ConstraintFormulaResultBinds?
Energy, over a 0.8 Hz drop 2 Ekin df / f0 2×5600×0.8/50 = 179.2 MJ = 49.8 kWh No
Power headroom, at 1 Hz/s 2 Ekin RoCoF / f0 2×5600×1/50 = 224 MW Yes
Current, on top of 1.0 pu dispatch 0.20 pu + 1.0 pu dispatch 1.2 pu = Imax Yes

Energy: take the 1120 MVA fleet as batteries with one hour of storage, at unity power factor so that 1120 MVA carries 1120 MW assumed for this case. It holds 1120 MW × 1 h = 1120 MWh = 1,120,000 kWh, 22,500 times the 49.8 kWh required. Energy does not bind because the nadir event lasts about 10 s and the store lasts 3600 s. (The 179.2 MJ figure is linearized in df; the exact release, Ekin[1 − (1 − df/f0)²], is 177.8 MJ, 0.8% lower.) Power: at 1 Hz/s assumed for this case (see the model-limits note on RoCoF figures below), a converter running virtual inertia Hv = 5 s delivers 2Hv RoCoF/f0 = 2×5×1/50 = 0.20 pu of its own rating (this reuses the converter model and the Park amplitude-invariant, d-axis-on-measured-voltage convention fixed earlier in this series, not re-derived here), so 224 MW needs 224/0.20 = 1120 MVA of grid-forming plant. Current: that same 0.20 pu, stacked on 1.0 pu dispatch current, uses 1.2 pu — exactly Imax = 1.2 pu assumed for this case, with nothing left over. Adding the two currents as plain magnitudes assumes both are in phase with the voltage (unity-power-factor dispatch).

Power headroom and current limit bind together at 1120 MVA; energy does not bind at all. The three rows are never combined into one number — MJ, MW, and pu current are different physical quantities, and "the largest of the three" would be sized by nothing meaningful. Comparing them separately is exactly what identifies headroom and current as the binding pair.

The code

The first listing integrates the aggregate nadir equation with fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4). Its global error falls as the fourth power of the step, against the first power for the forward Euler of Lesson 1. Here the right-hand side depends on t only, so each RK4 step is Simpson's rule, which is exact for the linear ramp; the listing's nadirs therefore equal the closed form up to floating-point rounding. It sweeps Ekin,sys over the four cases in Fig. 8.1, bisects for the smallest value that keeps the nadir at or above 48.8 Hz, then reproduces Exercise 2's sweep over T.

"""Lesson 8: aggregated nadir model, RK4 integration, and inertia sizing.

Swing convention (system-wide, centre-of-inertia form):
    2 E_kin_sys / f_0 * d(df)/dt = dP(t)
State: df (Hz), integrated by RK4 on a linear primary-response ramp.
"""
F0 = 50.0  # Hz, nominal frequency


def dp_ramp(t, dp0, ramp_t):
    """Net imbalance power (MW) at time t: loss dp0, ramped away by T."""
    if t >= ramp_t:
        return 0.0
    return -dp0 + dp0 * t / ramp_t


def rk4_nadir(e_kin_sys, dp0, ramp_t, dt=0.001, t_end=None):
    """Integrate d(df)/dt = f0 * dp(t) / (2 * e_kin_sys); return min df."""
    if t_end is None:
        t_end = ramp_t
    coef = F0 / (2.0 * e_kin_sys)

    def deriv(t):
        return coef * dp_ramp(t, dp0, ramp_t)

    t = 0.0
    df = 0.0
    df_min = 0.0
    n = int(round(t_end / dt))
    for i in range(n):
        k1 = deriv(t)
        k2 = deriv(t + dt / 2.0)
        k3 = deriv(t + dt / 2.0)
        k4 = deriv(t + dt)
        df = df + (dt / 6.0) * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)
        t = t + dt
        if df < df_min:
            df_min = df
    return df_min


def rocof0(e_kin_sys, dp0):
    return F0 * dp0 / (2.0 * e_kin_sys)


def bisect_floor(dp0, ramp_t, f_floor_drop, lo=10.0, hi=500.0, tol=1e-4):
    """Smallest E_kin_sys (GVA.s) keeping |nadir drop| <= f_floor_drop."""
    def drop_at(e):
        return -rk4_nadir(e * 1000.0, dp0, ramp_t)  # GVA.s -> MVA.s

    # drop_at is decreasing in e; find root drop_at(e) = f_floor_drop
    while hi - lo > tol:
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if drop_at(mid) > f_floor_drop:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return hi


def main():
    dp0 = 1000.0  # MW
    ramp_t = 10.0  # s

    print("RoCoF_0 and nadir vs E_kin_sys (dP=1000 MW, T=10 s):")
    for e_gva in (300.0, 200.0, 100.0, 50.0):
        e_mva = e_gva * 1000.0  # GVA.s -> MVA.s
        rc0 = rocof0(e_mva, dp0)
        drop = rk4_nadir(e_mva, dp0, ramp_t)
        f_nadir = F0 + drop
        print(f"  E_kin_sys = {e_gva:6.1f} GVA.s  "
              f"RoCoF_0 = {rc0:.3f} Hz/s  "
              f"f_nadir = {f_nadir:.3f} Hz")

    # Floor for 48.8 Hz (drop of 1.2 Hz)
    floor = bisect_floor(dp0, ramp_t, 1.2)
    print(f"\nSmallest E_kin_sys keeping nadir >= 48.8 Hz: "
          f"{floor:.1f} GVA.s")

    # analytic check: E_floor = f0 dP T / (4 * 1.2)
    analytic = F0 * dp0 * ramp_t / (4.0 * 1.2) / 1000.0  # -> GVA.s
    print(f"Analytic formula f0 dP T / (4 * 1.2): {analytic:.1f} GVA.s")

    # check at 100 GVA.s directly
    drop100 = rk4_nadir(100.0 * 1000.0, dp0, ramp_t)
    print(f"\nCheck at 100 GVA.s: f_nadir = {F0 + drop100:.3f} Hz "
          f"(below 48.8 Hz line: {F0 + drop100 < 48.8})")

    # Exercise 2: sweep T at 200 GVA.s
    print("\nExercise 2: sweep T at E_kin_sys = 200 GVA.s")
    for t_ramp in (5.0, 10.0, 20.0, 30.0):
        drop = rk4_nadir(200.0 * 1000.0, dp0, t_ramp)
        print(f"  T = {t_ramp:4.1f} s  df_nadir = {drop:.4f} Hz")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

The second listing builds the 4-by-4 state matrix from the key derivation and asks numpy.linalg.eigvals — the one numpy call this lesson's house rules allow — for its eigenvalues, then checks the relative mode's frequency and damping ratio.

"""Lesson 8: two-unit state matrix and its eigenvalues.

State x = (delta_1, delta_2, dw_1, dw_2). System base 1000 MVA, 50 Hz.
Swing convention: M_i d(dw_i)/dt = P_m - P_e - K_D dw_i,
                  M_i = 2 H_i S_i / S_sys (system base),
                  d(delta)/dt = omega_0 dw   (dw in pu, omega_0 = 2*pi*f0)
"""
import math
import numpy as np

F0 = 50.0
OMEGA0 = 2.0 * math.pi * F0

M1, M2 = 3.2, 1.8      # s, system-base inertia (2*H*S/S_sys)
KD1, KD2 = 0.8, 0.6     # pu on system base
E1, E2 = 1.05, 1.05
X12 = 2.0               # pu
P_TRANSFER = 0.2        # pu, pre-disturbance transfer


def build_state_matrix(delta12):
    """4x4 state matrix for x = (d1, d2, dw1, dw2), as nested lists."""
    k12 = (E1 * E2 / X12) * math.cos(delta12)  # pu/rad
    a = [
        [0.0, 0.0, OMEGA0, 0.0],
        [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, OMEGA0],
        [-k12 / M1, k12 / M1, -KD1 / M1, 0.0],
        [k12 / M2, -k12 / M2, 0.0, -KD2 / M2],
    ]
    return a, k12


def main():
    delta12 = math.asin(P_TRANSFER * X12 / (E1 * E2))
    print(f"delta_12 = {math.degrees(delta12):.2f} deg")

    a, k12 = build_state_matrix(delta12)
    print(f"K_12 = {k12:.4f} pu/rad")

    eigs = np.linalg.eigvals(a)
    eigs = sorted(eigs, key=lambda z: (abs(z.imag), z.real))
    print("Eigenvalues (1/s):")
    seen = set()
    for z in eigs:
        key = (round(z.real, 6), round(abs(z.imag), 6))
        if key in seen:
            continue
        seen.add(key)
        if abs(z.imag) < 1e-8:
            print(f"  {z.real:.4f}")
        else:
            print(f"  {z.real:.4f} +/- j{abs(z.imag):.2f}")

    common_expected = -(KD1 + KD2) / (M1 + M2)
    print(f"\nExpected common mode -(KD1+KD2)/(M1+M2) = "
          f"{common_expected:.4f} 1/s")

    omega_n = math.sqrt(OMEGA0 * k12 * (1.0 / M1 + 1.0 / M2))
    print(f"Check omega_n = sqrt(omega0*K12*(1/M1+1/M2)) = "
          f"{omega_n:.2f} rad/s")

    rel = [z for z in eigs if abs(z.imag) > 1e-8][0]
    f_rel = abs(rel.imag) / (2.0 * math.pi)
    zeta = -rel.real / abs(rel)
    print(f"Relative mode frequency = {f_rel:.3f} Hz")
    print(f"Relative mode damping ratio = {zeta:.4f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

The state matrix is built once from delta12 and passed straight to numpy.linalg.eigvals as a plain nested list — numpy is used only for that one eigenvalue call, everything else is math. The independent check (omega_n = 11.8358 rad/s against the eigenvalue's magnitude of 11.8358 rad/s; the printed 11.84 against the imaginary part's 11.83 is a rounding split, since the damped frequency 11.8348 rad/s is 0.001 rad/s lower) and the common-mode formula check (−0.2800 against −0.280003) both agree with the solver to four decimals, which is what makes the classification into "common mode" and "relative mode" more than a label — both have a second, hand-derivable formula backing them.

What this model captures, and what it misses

  • One aggregated mass for the nadir. The nadir formula treats the system as one rotor on Ekin,sys. Real machines do not swing perfectly together after a large loss — that is the relative mode the two-unit eigenvalue analysis exposes. The aggregated nadir is a centre-of-inertia answer; individual machines can dip lower, or recover faster, than it says.
  • No load damping. This lesson's nadir derivation sets KD = 0 for the aggregate equation (load reduces its own demand as frequency falls, an effect this series names Dload, never bare D). Exercise 3 adds it back and shows the nadir gets shallower.
  • A linear ramp stands in for governor and converter response, not the real dynamics of either (Anderson and Mirheydar 1990) cited [S13 — verify], the standard reference for this class of approximation and its limits.
  • Two units stand in for a network. A real grid has many machines and tie lines, with more than one relative mode; two units is the smallest system that shows the mechanism at all.
  • Converter stability while saturated at Imax is not proved here, and is an open problem (National Grid ESO GC0137; IEEE Std 2800-2022) cited [S17, S18 — verify]. Sizing a fleet to sit exactly at Imax says nothing about whether it stays stable once actually saturated there.
  • Two different rate-of-change-of-frequency figures, kept separate, on purpose. The 1 Hz/s figure used in the sizing case above is a design value for this case, close to the figure used in Ireland and in Great Britain loss-of-mains protection policy cited [S16 — verify]. It is a different number, for a different purpose, from the ENTSO-E withstand guidance figure, reported as 2 Hz/s measured over 500 ms cited [S15 — verify]. This lesson never merges them into one figure and never writes "ENTSO-E 1 Hz/s" — that string names two different sources as one.

Exercises

1. (Derivational) Derive the nadir formula dfnadir = − f0 dP T / (4 Ekin,sys) from the aggregate swing equation, and say exactly where the factor 4 comes from.

Answer target

As in the key derivation: one factor of 2 is already present in the coefficient 2Ekin,sys/f0 on the left of the differential equation; the second factor of 2 comes from ∫0T(1 − t/T) dt = T/2 when the ramp is integrated from 0 to T. The nadir occurs at t = T because the right-hand side is exactly zero from then on.

2. (Computational) Sweep T over 5, 10, 20, 30 s at Ekin,sys = 200 GVA·s and report the nadir at each.

Answer target

The formula gives −0.3125, −0.625, −1.25, −1.875 Hz. The listing's own RK4 run above prints exactly these four values to four decimals, agreeing with the closed form. The agreement is exact, not a small-step effect: for a right-hand side that depends on t only, RK4 is Simpson's rule, and Simpson's rule integrates the linear ramp without error.

3. (Derivational) Add load damping Dload (MW/Hz) to the aggregate equation: (2 Ekin,sys / f0) d(df)/dt = dPramp(t) − Dload df. Show the nadir is shallower than the undamped case, and name the dimensionless group that decides when damping dominates the ramp.

Answer target

Proof that the nadir is shallower: let y = dfdamped − dfundamped and a = f0 Dload / (2 Ekin,sys) > 0. Subtracting the two equations gives dy/dt + a y = −a dfundamped ≥ 0, since dfundamped ≤ 0 for all t, with y(0) = 0. So y(t) ≥ 0 at every instant: the damped curve never goes below the undamped one, and its minimum is no lower. With damping the nadir also moves earlier than T: at t = T the right-hand side is −Dload df > 0, so frequency is already rising.

Group: Dload T f0 / (2 Ekin,sys) (MW/Hz × s × Hz / MJ cancels to a pure number). A probe run at Ekin,sys = 200 GVA·s, T = 10 s prints: group 0.0000, dfnadir = −0.6250 Hz at Dload = 0 (matches Exercise 2); group 0.6250, −0.4464 Hz at t = 7.77 s for Dload = 500 MW/Hz, already shallower; group 6.2500, −0.1366 Hz at t = 3.17 s for Dload = 5000 MW/Hz, where the group is far above 1 and damping dominates the ramp. Verify the group numerically, as this run does, before quoting it.

4. (Computational) In the two-unit listing, raise X12 from 2.0 to 4.0 pu at the same 0.2 pu transfer, and report the relative-mode frequency.

Answer target

At X12 = 4.0 pu the pre-disturbance angle widens to δ12,0 = 46.52° (the peak transfer E1E2/X12 falls from 0.5513 to 0.2756 pu, so a larger angle is needed to carry the same 0.2 pu transfer), and K12 falls from 0.5137 to 0.1897 pu/rad. Re-running the listing with this X12 prints a relative-mode frequency of 1.144 Hz, down from 1.884 Hz — it falls because K12 falls, and ωn ∝ √K12.

5. (Conceptual) Example 4.2's sizing case concludes that power headroom binds and energy does not. State one change to the case that reverses that conclusion.

Answer target

Any of: hold the response for minutes rather than seconds, which scales the energy requirement while leaving power and current unchanged; specify a converter fleet with no long-duration store behind it (photovoltaic generation without a battery, which supplies power for an instant but cannot sustain it); or set the requirement by a deep, sustained excursion rather than a rate of change — again energy-shaped, not power-shaped.

6. (Conceptual) Why is the zero eigenvalue of the two-unit model not an instability?

Answer target

Shifting both rotor angles δ1 and δ2 by the same constant changes no entry of the state matrix and no power flow, because the tie's power flow depends only on the angle difference δ1 − δ2, never on either angle by itself. There is no physical reference an absolute rotor angle is measured against, so a mode that does nothing but move that arbitrary reference is exactly what you should expect, not a sign that the system will run away.
