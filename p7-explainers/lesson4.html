Lesson 4 — Big Faults: the Equal-Area Criterion and a Bisection for Clearing Time

Series: single-machine transient stability Prerequisite: Lesson 2 (SMIB swing model), Lesson 3 (energy function)

1. Opening problem

Take the machine from Lesson 2: an inertia constant H = 3.5 s, synchronous speed ω0 = 2π·60 = 376.99 rad/s, internal voltage E′ = 1.1 pu behind its transient reactance, delivering mechanical power Pm = 0.8 pu into an infinite bus at V∞ = 1.0 pu. The path is the machine's transient reactance plus its step-up transformer, X′d + Xt = 0.45 pu, followed by two parallel lines, so the total pre-fault reactance is Xpre = 0.45 + 0.20 = 0.65 pu (the 0.20 pu is two 0.40 pu lines in parallel).

A bolted three-phase short circuit lands on line 2 at its sending-end bus, the bus that connects the transformer to both lines. That bus is now at zero voltage, and every path from the machine to the infinite bus goes through it, so no active power reaches the infinite bus: the machine's electrical output Pe is exactly zero in this model. Protection detects the fault and trips line 2, which removes it from service; the machine now delivers through X′d + Xt and the single surviving line, Xpost = 0.45 + 0.40 = 0.85 pu.

Question. How many milliseconds does protection have, from fault inception, to clear the fault before the machine can no longer recover synchronism?

Answer, computed below: 170 ms, that is 10.2 cycles at 60 Hz. Typical transmission protection clears a fault in 4 to 6 cycles [unsourced: ../p1-textbook/book-plan.md Example 1.2 states this range with no S-identifier; check it against S01 (Kundur 1994) or S04 (Anderson and Fouad 2003) — verify], so this machine has margin: at 5 cycles (83.3 ms), the margin is 170.1 − 83.3 = 86.8 ms on the two rounded figures, or 86.7 ms from the unrounded run (Exercise 4 works this out in full).

2. Learning goals

  1. Model a fault as a switch between three networks: pre-fault, fault-on, post-fault.
  2. Derive the equal-area criterion from the energy function of Lesson 3.
  3. Compute the critical clearing angle and the critical clearing time in closed form.
  4. Find the same critical clearing time by bisection over simulations, and compare.
  5. Explain why a small-signal eigenvalue says nothing about this question.

3. Model: three networks, one machine

Lesson 2 fixed the swing convention this whole series uses, and Lesson 4 keeps it without change:

swing equation 2H d(Δω)/dt = Pm − Pe − KDΔω,    dδ/dt = ω0Δω

Here δ is the rotor angle in radians, measured against a synchronously rotating reference; Δω is the per-unit speed deviation from synchronous speed; H is the inertia constant in seconds; KD is a damping coefficient in per-unit torque per per-unit speed deviation (never written D, to avoid confusion with a damping torque coefficient in different units); Pm is mechanical power in per unit and Pe is electrical power in per unit, both on the machine's own base. The machine is classical: constant E′ behind constant X′d (direct-axis transient reactance), with δ the angle of E′ relative to the infinite-bus voltage. For a single machine against an infinite bus through a pure reactance X, the electrical power is Pe = Pmax sinδ, with Pmax = E′V∞ / X.

This lesson's whole point is that a fault does not change the equations above; it changes X, and therefore Pmax, in three stages:

  • Pre-fault: both lines in service, Xpre = 0.65 pu, Pmax,pre = 1.1·1.0 / 0.65 = 1.6923 pu. The machine sits at the equilibrium angle where Pe = Pm: δ0 = arcsin(Pm / Pmax,pre) = arcsin(0.4727) = 0.4924 rad = 28.2°.
  • Fault-on: a bolted three-phase fault at the sending-end bus of the lines puts that bus at zero voltage. The network is purely reactive and every machine-to-infinite-bus path passes through that bus, so Pe = 0 exactly for the whole time the fault persists. This is the strongest assumption in the lesson, and Section 7 returns to it.
  • Post-fault: protection has cleared the fault by opening the breakers on the faulted line, which stays out of service. Only the transformer and the one surviving line carry power: Xpost = 0.85 pu, Pmax,post = 1.1·1.0 / 0.85 = 1.2941 pu. The post-fault stable equilibrium is δs,post = arcsin(0.8 / 1.2941) = 0.6664 rad = 38.18°.

Figure 4.1 draws all three Pe(δ) curves on one axis. Removing a line lowers the curve: the pre-fault curve peaks at 1.6923 pu, the post-fault curve at only 1.2941 pu, because one 0.40 pu line in place of two in parallel (0.20 pu) raises the total reactance from 0.65 pu to 0.85 pu, and Pmax = E′V∞ / X falls by the ratio 0.65/0.85 = 0.765. The fault-on curve is flat on the axis, at zero, because the zero-voltage fault bus lets no active power through to the infinite bus for as long as the fault lasts.

4. Derivation: the equal-area criterion

Set KD = 0 for this derivation — no damping during the fast swing following a big fault, an assumption Section 7 revisits. Multiply the swing equation by Δω and use dδ/dt = ω0Δω to trade the time variable for the angle variable:

from Lesson 3's energy function 2H Δω d(Δω)/dt = (Pm − Pe)Δω  ⇒  H d(Δω²)/dt = (1/ω0)(Pm − Pe) dδ/dt

Integrating both sides over time, from a start where Δω = 0 at angle δ0 to any later angle δ, gives the energy relation quoted in Lesson 3 and used here as the working tool of this lesson:

energy balance Hω0Δω² = ∫δ₀δ (Pm − Pe) dδ

The left side is ω0 times HΔω², and HΔω² is the kinetic energy of the speed deviation in per-unit seconds on the machine base (½J(ω0Δω)² divided by the rating, since H = ½Jω0² / rating). The factor ω0 appears because the right side is integrated over angle in radians. The left side can never be negative. The right side is the net area between the mechanical-power line and the electrical power curve, swept from δ0 to δ. While the fault is on, Pe = 0, so every degree of rotor swing adds positive area — the rotor gains kinetic energy monotonically, which is exactly why a fault accelerates the machine. Call the area accumulated up to the clearing angle δc the accelerating area:

accelerating area Aacc = ∫δ₀δc Pm dδ = Pm(δc − δ0)

The moment protection clears the fault, the network switches to the post-fault curve, Pe = Pmax,post sinδ. The post-fault curve delivers more than Pm only between δs,post = 38.18° and δmax = 141.82° (Figure 4.1 shows the orange curve crossing the 0.8 pu line at 38.18°). Between δ0 = 28.2° and 38.18° it still delivers less than Pm. The clearing angles of interest here (near 63.9°) lie inside the band, so after clearing the machine decelerates. Angle keeps increasing (speed is still positive) while area keeps subtracting from the kinetic energy already banked. Define the decelerating area swept from the clearing angle up to some later angle δ:

decelerating area Adec(δ) = ∫δcδ (Pmax,post sinδ − Pm) dδ

The machine's speed deviation returns to zero — the rotor stops advancing and starts to swing back — the first time Adec catches up to Aacc. The largest decelerating area the post-fault curve can ever supply, no matter how far δ is allowed to run, is the area up to δmax, the second angle on the post-fault curve where Pe = Pm again (the unstable equilibrium of the post-fault system):

far equilibrium on the post-fault curve δmax = π − arcsin(Pm / Pmax,post) = π − arcsin(0.8 / 1.2941) = 2.4752 rad = 141.82°

Beyond δmax, the post-fault curve falls below Pm again and every further degree adds kinetic energy instead of removing it: the rotor accelerates away and never comes back. So the criterion for return is exactly this: the clearing angle δc is small enough that Aacc ≤ Adec(δmax). The boundary case, where the machine only just returns — arriving at δmax with exactly zero speed deviation left — defines the critical clearing angle δcr, and the boundary condition is the equal-area criterion:

equal-area criterion Aacc = Adec(δmax)

Substituting the two area formulas at δc = δcr and carrying out both integrals:

Pm(δcr − δ0) = Pmax,post[cosδcr − cosδmax] − Pm(δmax − δcr)

Move the term −Pm(δmax − δcr) to the left. The two linear terms add to Pm(δmax − δ0): the +Pmδcr and −Pmδcr terms cancel. The only remaining δcr dependence is cosδcr, which gives a single equation:

critical clearing angle, closed form cosδcr = [Pm(δmax − δ0) + Pmax,post cosδmax] / Pmax,post

Plugging in the numbers already on hand (Pm = 0.8, δmax = 2.4752 rad, δ0 = 0.4924 rad, Pmax,post = 1.2941 pu, cosδmax = −0.7860):

cosδcr = 0.4397  ⇒  δcr = 1.1155 rad = 63.92°

5. Closed-form critical clearing time

An angle is not yet a time. To turn δcr into a clearing time, use the fact that during the fault Pe = 0 exactly, so the swing equation (still KD = 0) is a constant acceleration:

2H d(Δω)/dt = Pm  ⇒  Δω(t) = Pmt / (2H)

using Δω(0) = 0 at fault inception. Substituting into dδ/dt = ω0Δω and integrating once more from δ(0) = δ0:

quadratic swing during the fault δ(t) = δ0 + ω0Pmt² / (4H)

Setting δ(t) = δcr and solving for t gives the critical clearing time:

critical clearing time, closed form tcr = √[4H(δcr − δ0) / (ω0Pm)] = √[4·3.5·0.6231 / (376.99·0.8)] = 0.1701 s = 10.2 cycles at 60 Hz

That is the 170 ms quoted in the opening problem. It is exact for this model — a bolted fault, zero damping, one machine against an infinite bus — because both the parabola for the fault-on swing and the equal-area boundary for the post-fault return are themselves exact consequences of the same swing equation, with no approximation beyond the modelling assumptions of Section 7. Unrounded, the value is 0.17008 s.

6. Code: the same answer by bisection

lesson4_cct.py checks the closed-form result against a direct simulation. A function stable(t_clear) integrates the fault-on system (Pe = 0) with fourth-order Runge–Kutta at a fixed step h = 0.5 ms up to tclear, switches the network to the post-fault curve, continues to t = 5 s, and returns False the moment δ passes δmax. A bisection then searches tclear ∈ [0.10, 0.25] s for the boundary between True (returns) and False (runs away), stopping once the bracket is 1 ms wide or narrower. The listing below is the actual file, run with python lesson4_cct.py; the output beneath it is the real, unedited stdout of that run.

"""Equal-area criterion and bisection for critical clearing time.

Swing convention: 2H d(dw)/dt = P_m - P_e - K_D dw
                   d(delta)/dt = omega_0 dw
Stdlib only. Run: python lesson4_cct.py
"""
import math

H = 3.5
OMEGA_0 = 2.0 * math.pi * 60.0
E_P = 1.1
V_INF = 1.0
X_PRE = 0.65
X_POST = 0.45 + 0.40
P_M = 0.8

P_MAX_PRE = E_P * V_INF / X_PRE
P_MAX_POST = E_P * V_INF / X_POST


def f(x, p_max, k_d):
    """State derivative. x = (delta, dw). p_max = 0 during the fault."""
    delta, dw = x
    ddelta = OMEGA_0 * dw
    ddw = (P_M - p_max * math.sin(delta) - k_d * dw) / (2.0 * H)
    return (ddelta, ddw)


def rk4_step(x, p_max, k_d, h):
    k1 = f(x, p_max, k_d)
    x2 = (x[0] + 0.5 * h * k1[0], x[1] + 0.5 * h * k1[1])
    k2 = f(x2, p_max, k_d)
    x3 = (x[0] + 0.5 * h * k2[0], x[1] + 0.5 * h * k2[1])
    k3 = f(x3, p_max, k_d)
    x4 = (x[0] + h * k3[0], x[1] + h * k3[1])
    k4 = f(x4, p_max, k_d)
    dd = (k1[0] + 2 * k2[0] + 2 * k3[0] + k4[0]) / 6.0
    dw = (k1[1] + 2 * k2[1] + 2 * k3[1] + k4[1]) / 6.0
    return (x[0] + h * dd, x[1] + h * dw)


def stable(t_clear, delta0, delta_max, k_d=0.0, h=0.5e-3, t_end=5.0):
    """True if delta never crosses delta_max after clearing at t_clear."""
    x = (delta0, 0.0)
    t = 0.0
    while t < t_clear - 1e-12:
        x = rk4_step(x, 0.0, k_d, h)
        t += h
    while t < t_end - 1e-12:
        x = rk4_step(x, P_MAX_POST, k_d, h)
        t += h
        if x[0] > delta_max:
            return False
    return True


def bisect_tcr(delta0, delta_max, k_d=0.0, lo=0.10, hi=0.25, tol=1e-3):
    """Bisection over t_clear; lo must be stable, hi must be unstable."""
    assert stable(lo, delta0, delta_max, k_d)
    assert not stable(hi, delta0, delta_max, k_d)
    while hi - lo > tol:
        mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
        if stable(mid, delta0, delta_max, k_d):
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return lo, hi

# ... main() computes the closed form, runs three bisections
#     (K_D = 0 at 1 ms and 0.1 ms resolution, and K_D = 2 pu),
#     and prints the protection-margin arithmetic of Exercise 4.

The bisection bracket [0.1697, 0.1703] s straddles the closed-form value 0.1701 s. The two methods agree because both neglect damping: the equal-area criterion is derived with KD = 0, and so is this run of the simulation. The resolution used is h = 0.5 ms for the RK4 step and a 1 ms bisection tolerance. One detail of stable() limits any tighter bisection: the fault-on loop takes whole steps while t < tclear, so the network actually switches at tclear rounded up to the next multiple of 0.5 ms. Every tclear in (0.1695, 0.1700] s gives the same run (switch at 0.1700 s, stable), and every value in (0.1700, 0.1705] s gives the same run (switch at 0.1705 s, unstable). The simulated boundary is therefore known only to lie between switching at 0.1700 s and at 0.1705 s, a 0.5 ms interval that contains the closed-form 0.17008 s. Exercise 2 shows what happens when the bisection tolerance goes below the step.

7. Model limits

  • No damping during the swing. KD = 0 throughout Sections 4 and 5. Exercise 3 adds KD = 2 pu back into the simulation and finds a longer critical clearing time, 0.1762–0.1768 s. The damping torque KDΔω opposes the acceleration during the fault and adds to the deceleration after clearing, so a longer fault is needed to reach the stability boundary. The closed-form derivation of Sections 4–5 does not extend to KD ≠ 0: the neat cancellation that produced the closed-form cosδcr relies on the energy integral being path-independent, which damping breaks.
  • A classical machine. E′ and X′ are held constant; no excitation system, no governor, no saturation. Real generators have all three, and on a longer time horizon than the first swing they change the picture.
  • A single machine against an infinite bus. The infinite bus fixes V∞ and its own angle, so this whole lesson reduces the network to one algebraic equation, Pe = Pmax sinδ. Two real machines swinging against each other reduce to the same single-machine form only after their angles are combined; more than two machines generally do not reduce at all.
  • A bolted fault with Pe = 0 exactly. A three-phase short circuit with zero fault impedance, located electrically at the machine terminal, is the worst case this model can represent; a fault through some nonzero impedance, or on a remote bus, leaves Pe > 0 during the fault and gives the machine a longer critical clearing time than the one computed here.

Multi-machine transient stability needs time-domain simulation. Equal area is exact only for one machine against an infinite bus, or for two machines whose swing equations reduce algebraically to that same single-angle form. For three or more machines with distinct inertias and network couplings, there is no single (Pm, Pe, δ) picture left to draw an area on, so the industry practice for a real multi-machine network is exactly the RK4-style time simulation this lesson used to check the closed form, not the closed form itself.

Why the Lesson 2 eigenvalues say nothing here. The eigenvalues computed in Lesson 2, λ = −0.1429 ± j8.961 rad/s (Example 1.1 of ../p1-textbook/book-plan.md, which prints j8.962; the damped imaginary part from unrounded Ks is 8.9607, with KD = 2 pu), come from linearising the swing equation about the pre-fault equilibrium angle δ0 = 28.2°, where the synchronising coefficient is Ks = Pmax,precosδ0 = 1.4913 pu/rad. They describe how the machine responds to an infinitesimal nudge away from that one point. The post-fault network has its own equilibrium, δs,post = 38.18°, with Ks,post = 1.2941·cos 38.18° = 1.0172 pu/rad; the same formula λ = −KD/(4H) ± j√[ω0Ks/(2H) − (KD/(4H))²] gives λpost = −0.1429 ± j7.400 rad/s. Both equilibria are small-signal stable (negative real parts), and neither set of eigenvalues says whether the rotor gets from the first equilibrium to the second after a fault. This lesson's fault does not nudge the rotor by an infinitesimal amount: it drives δ from 28.2° toward, and past, 63.9° and potentially all the way to 141.8°, a large excursion in which sinδ is nowhere close to its linear approximation sinδ0 + cosδ0(δ − δ0). A small-signal eigenvalue answers "does the machine settle after a small nudge"; it has no opinion on "does the machine come back after this fault", because that second question is decided by the full nonlinear sinδ term over a wide angular range, which is precisely what the equal-area criterion — and the RK4 simulation behind Figure 4.3 — account for and a linearised eigenvalue calculation cannot.

8. Exercises

1. (Derivational) Derive A_acc = A_dec from the energy function of Lesson 3.

Answer target

Start from Lesson 3's energy balance, Hω0Δω² = ∫δ₀δ(Pm − Pe)dδ, evaluated between two states where Δω = 0: the fault inception (Δω = 0 at δ0) and the return point (Δω = 0 again, at some angle up to δmax). Both left-hand sides are zero, so the right-hand side integrated over the whole excursion must be zero too: ∫δ₀δmax(Pm − Pe)dδ = 0. Split that integral at the clearing angle δc, where Pe switches from 0 to Pmax,postsinδ: the first piece is exactly Aacc (positive, since Pm > Pe = 0 there), and the second piece is exactly −Adec (negative, since Pe > Pm on the post-fault curve between δs,post = 38.18° and δmax = 141.82°, and the clearing angle 63.9° is inside that band). So Aacc − Adec = 0, which is Aacc = Adec.

2. (Computational) Tighten the bisection to 0.1 ms and report the bracket.

Target: report the run's bracket and the step used, then explain why the bracket does not contain the closed-form 0.17008 s.

Answer target

Run's bracket: [0.16995, 0.17002] s, with the RK4 step held at h = 0.5 ms (only the bisection tolerance changed, to 0.1 ms). The closed-form 0.17008 s is not inside this bracket: its upper end, 0.17002 s, is 0.06 ms short. The reason is the step grid, not an error in either method. stable() switches the network at tclear rounded up to a multiple of 0.5 ms, so the run at 0.16995 s actually clears at 0.1700 s (stable) and the run at 0.17002 s actually clears at 0.1705 s (unstable). The true information is "the boundary lies between 0.1700 s and 0.1705 s", which does contain 0.17008 s. A bisection tolerance below the step size h adds no information. To resolve the boundary more finely, reduce h: at h = 0.05 ms and a 0.01 ms tolerance the bracket is [0.170047, 0.170056] s, whose effective switching instants 0.17005 s and 0.17010 s bracket 0.17008 s.

3. (Computational) Add K_D = 2 pu to the simulation and report the new critical clearing time.

Target: report the value the run returns; it is longer than 0.170 s because damping removes energy.

Answer target

Run's bracket with KD = 2 pu: [0.1762, 0.1768] s, about 6 ms longer than the undamped 0.1701 s. Damping subtracts KDΔω from the accelerating torque during the fault-on swing, so the rotor accumulates less kinetic energy for a given clearing time. After clearing, the same term adds to the decelerating torque while Δω > 0, so the post-fault network removes that energy faster. Both effects make a longer fault necessary to reach the stability boundary. The effect is small because Δω is small: at 0.170 s it is 0.8·0.170/7 = 0.0194 pu, so the damping torque is only about 2·0.0194 = 0.039 pu against Pm = 0.8 pu.

4. (Conceptual) Protection clears in 5 cycles. State the time margin in milliseconds against t_cr.

Target: 5/60 = 83.3 ms, margin 170.1 − 83.3 = 86.8 ms, with the 4 to 6 cycle figure cited [verify].

Answer target

5 cycles at 60 Hz = 5/60 s = 0.0833 s = 83.3 ms. tcr = 0.1701 s = 170.1 ms. Rounding both to one decimal place and subtracting gives a margin of 170.1 − 83.3 = 86.8 ms; subtracting the unrounded run values gives 86.7 ms (the two differ only by rounding the intermediate figures before subtracting). 5. (Conceptual) Explain why this machine is small-signal stable both before the fault and after it is cleared, yet it can still be lost.

Target: eigenvalues describe small deviations about an equilibrium; a fault moves the state far from it, into the region where the nonlinear sin(delta) decides.

Answer target

The Lesson 2 eigenvalues, λ = −0.1429 ± j8.961 rad/s, come from linearising Pe = Pmaxsinδ about the pre-fault equilibrium δ0 = 28.2° (Ks = 1.4913 pu/rad, KD = 2 pu). Linearising about the post-fault equilibrium δs,post = 38.18° (Ks,post = 1.0172 pu/rad) gives λpost = −0.1429 ± j7.400 rad/s. Both pairs have negative real parts, so both equilibria are small-signal stable. Each pair says only how the machine responds to an infinitesimally small deviation from its own point. But a big fault does not produce an infinitesimal deviation: it drives δ from 28.2° up through 63.9° and potentially to 141.8°, an excursion over which sinδ departs sharply from its tangent line at δ0. Whether the machine returns is decided by the full nonlinear energy balance of Section 4 — the equal-area criterion — not by the local slope at one point. A system can be small-signal stable (decaying eigenvalues) at both its pre-fault and post-fault equilibria and still lose synchronism during the large excursion that connects them, because the linearisation never sees the excursion at all.
