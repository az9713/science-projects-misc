Lesson 1 — Frequency Is Shared State: The Swing Equation as One Integrator

Series: Power-system dynamics for programmers. Lesson 1 of 8. This lesson treats grid frequency as the output of one integrator driven by a power imbalance, derives that integrator from Newton's law for a spinning rotor, and runs the integrator in twenty-five lines of Python.

The opening problem

A 50 Hz grid stores Ekin,sys = 200 GVA·s of kinetic energy in the spinning mass of its machines assumed for this lesson — if you attach this number to a real grid, cite it with a year and mark it verify. A 1000 MW power station trips instantly. No governor, no primary response, and no automatic load shedding acts. How many seconds pass until frequency reaches 49.2 Hz, and how many until it reaches 48.8 Hz?

Answer, computed by this lesson: frequency falls at a constant 0.125 Hz/s, so it takes 6.40 s to reach 49.2 Hz and 9.60 s to reach 48.8 Hz. The rest of the lesson builds the one equation that produces those two numbers, and a program that checks them.

49.2 Hz and 48.8 Hz are in the frequency band at which grid operators stage automatic actions — load shedding, generator trips, protection relays. The time to reach such a threshold sets the time budget for a control action: an action that arrives later than that time cannot prevent the threshold action. This lesson shows that, with no control acting, this time is proportional to the kinetic energy spinning on the grid at the moment of the disturbance and inversely proportional to the power lost.

The model and its symbols

An AC grid connects many generators through wires. Every generator's rotor turns at a mechanical speed tied to the grid's electrical frequency by its number of poles. If two generators ran at different average electrical frequencies for any length of time, the phase angle between their internal voltages would drift without bound, and the power flowing between them (which depends on the sine of that angle) would swing from motoring to generating every time the angle passed through a multiple of 180°. No physical grid can sustain that, so — to first order, and while the grid stays connected — every synchronous machine on the same AC grid must run at the same average electrical frequency. That is the central claim of this lesson: on the timescale of seconds, frequency is not "a property of each machine." It is one shared state variable, common to the whole interconnected system.

Because it is one shared quantity, the question "what happens to frequency after a disturbance" can be answered by one aggregated equation, before any machine, network, or converter model is introduced individually. That equation is the swing equation, and this lesson derives it, aggregates it, and turns it into a program.

Per-unit quantities

Power-system quantities are conventionally expressed in per unit (p.u.): every physical quantity is divided by a chosen base quantity of the same dimension, so that "1.0 p.u." means "at rated value." Two bases are chosen (Sbase and Vbase); the other two follow from circuit laws:

  • Sbase — base apparent power (MVA), usually the rated power of the piece of equipment or the system under study.
  • Vbase — base line-to-line voltage (kV).
  • Ibase — base current (A), derived as Ibase = Sbase / (√3 · Vbase).
  • Zbase — base impedance (Ω), derived as Zbase = Vbase² / Sbase.

The payoff of per unit is not cosmetic. A transformer changes voltage by its turns ratio, so a quantity expressed in volts or amps changes value crossing every transformer in a network — bookkeeping that a hand or a program must track winding by winding. Choose the base voltages on the two sides of an ideal transformer in the same ratio as its rated turns ratio, and the transformer's per-unit impedance is the same seen from either side: the ratio disappears from the equations entirely. That is why the powers P and the speed deviation Δω in the swing equation below are in per unit, and H is in seconds on the machine's own Sbase: the equation can then be applied to a machine, a plant, or an entire interconnected system without re-deriving it for each voltage level.

The inertia constant H

A rotor of moment of inertia J (kg·m²), turning at its rated mechanical angular speed ωm0 (rad/s), stores kinetic energy Ekin = ½ J ωm0². The inertia constant H is that stored energy divided by the machine's own base power:

H = Ekin / Sbase  =  ½ J ωm0² / Sbase

H has the unit of seconds (Exercise 4 asks you to show this), and it has a direct physical reading: it is the number of seconds the machine's own rated power could be supplied purely from its own stored kinetic energy, with no other source, before that energy ran out. Typical turbo-generator values are 2 s to 8 s cited [S01 — verify]. For a turbo-set, J is the total of the generator rotor and every turbine stage on the same shaft. Exercise 1 computes a worked example.

The swing-equation symbols

The remaining symbols, all defined before they are used in the derivation below:

  • ω, ω0 — electrical angular speed and its rated value, ω0 = 2π f0 (rad/s).
  • Δω (written dw in code) — the per-unit speed deviation, Δω = (ω − ω0) / ω0.
  • δ — the rotor angle (rad, electrical), measured relative to a frame rotating at ω0.
  • Pm, Pe — mechanical power in and electrical power out, in per unit.
  • KD — a per-unit damping coefficient (never called D in this series — see below).

The key derivation

Start from Newton's second law for rotation applied to one rotor:

J dωm/dt  =  Tm − Te

with Tm and Te the mechanical and electrical torques (N·m) and ωm the mechanical angular speed. Multiply both sides by ωm and use power = torque × speed, P = T ωm:

J ωm dωm/dt  =  Pm − Pe

Near an electromechanical disturbance, ωm stays close to its rated value ωm0 (a few percent deviation at most before protection acts), so ωm on the left can be replaced by the constant ωm0 without materially changing the equation — this is the small-deviation assumption behind every swing-equation model in this series. Next, write the speed in per unit. The electrical speed is ω = p ωm, with p the number of pole pairs, so the per-unit deviation is the same for mechanical and electrical speed: Δω = (ωm − ωm0) / ωm0, and therefore dωm/dt = ωm0 d(Δω)/dt. Substitute this and divide both sides by the machine's base power Sbase:

(J ωm0² / Sbase) d(Δω)/dt  =  Pm − Pe  (per unit)

From the definition above, J ωm0² / Sbase = 2H. With a damping term added (next paragraph), the result — derived here — is the per-unit swing equation used through this whole series:

2H d(Δω)/dt  =  Pm − Pe − KD Δω
dδ/dt  =  ω0 Δω

The damping term KD Δω is added here by convention, representing effects — turbine friction, damper windings, frequency-sensitive load — that oppose a speed deviation. This series always writes that coefficient as KD, multiplying the per-unit speed deviation Δω. A second convention exists in the literature, where a damping torque is instead written proportional to the speed deviation in electrical rad/s and called D, so that KD = ω0 D; this lesson names that alternative once and does not use it — mixing the two conventions inside one model multiplies the damping coefficient, and so the damping ratio, by ω0 — a factor of 314 at 50 Hz (or its reciprocal). A D quoted per mechanical rad/s adds a further factor of the pole-pair count.

Read as a block diagram, the frequency dynamics is one integrator with one feedback path: the power imbalance Pm − Pe, minus a damping signal fed back from the output, is scaled by 1/(2H) and integrated to produce Δω; that in turn is scaled by ω0 and integrated again to produce the rotor angle δ. The second integrator is downstream: in this isolated model δ does not feed back into Δω (in Lesson 2 it does, through Pe). Figure 1.1 draws this signal-flow structure.

Fig. 1.1. The swing equation drawn as signal flow: a power imbalance enters a summing junction, is integrated once (gain 1/2H) to the per-unit speed deviation Δω, fed back through a damping gain KD, and integrated a second time (gain ω0) to the rotor angle δ. In this model Δω is the integral of the power imbalance minus the damping signal, scaled by 1/2H; it has no other input.

Aggregating the whole grid

Every synchronous machine on the grid obeys its own copy of the swing equation, all sharing the same Δω because — as argued above — they run at the same frequency. Summing the individual equations of N machines (index i), each with its own Hi and rating Si (MVA), after multiplying each machine's equation by Si to convert its per-unit powers to MW (with KD = 0):

Σi 2Hi Si d(Δω)/dt  =  Σi (Pm,i − Pe,i) Si

This is one system-wide integrator with total stored energy

Ekin,sys  =  Σi Hi Si  (MW·s)

The sum runs over the machines still connected after the disturbance: the tripped station's rotor leaves the system, so the 200 GVA·s of the opening problem is the stored energy after the trip.

Convert the per-unit deviation to hertz with Δf = f0 Δω (written df in code). Specialize to a single step loss of generation ΔP (MW, written DP in code) with load unchanged and no governor response, so the right-hand side of the summed equation is −ΔP (the case of the opening problem). The swing equation reduces to:

d(Δf)/dt  =  −f0 ΔP / (2 Ekin,sys)

The right-hand side is a constant, so this is the simplest possible integrator: a fixed slope. That constant slope is the rate of change of frequency, RoCoF:

RoCoF0  =  f0 ΔP / (2 Ekin,sys)  =  50 × 1000 / (2 × 200000)  =  0.125 Hz/s

derived here, with f0 in Hz, ΔP in MW, and Ekin,sys in MW·s (200 GVA·s = 200,000 MW·s). This is the whole answer to the opening problem: the grid's frequency falls in direct proportion to the size of the power lost and in inverse proportion to how much kinetic energy is spinning at the moment it is lost. RoCoF0 is defined here as a positive magnitude; the frequency falls, so d(Δf)/dt = −RoCoF0. The single assumption that makes the aggregation valid — treating every machine's Δω as the same number — is that all machines swing together as one coherent mass during the seconds that matter. Lesson 8 relaxes exactly that assumption and shows machines that do not swing together.

The code

The listing below integrates the constant-slope equation above with forward Euler and reports the first time step at which frequency crosses each threshold. Forward Euler updates the state by Δf ← Δf − RoCoF0 · h at every step of size h = H_STEP. The code variable rocof0 carries the sign, rocof0 = −RoCoF0, so the code prints −0.125. Because the right-hand side here does not depend on Δf or t — it is a plain constant — forward Euler makes zero truncation error at every step: the numerical answer equals the closed form to floating-point precision. Lesson 3 uses this same solver on an equation whose right-hand side is not constant, and shows the same solver drifting from the true answer.

"""RoCoF integrator for the L1 opening problem (forward Euler)."""
F0 = 50.0
E_KIN = 200e3
DP = 1000.0
H_STEP = 0.01
EPS = 1e-9

rocof0 = -F0 * DP / (2.0 * E_KIN)
df = 0.0
n = 0
t_92 = None
t_88 = None
while n < 2000:
    df += rocof0 * H_STEP
    n += 1
    t = n * H_STEP
    if t_92 is None and df <= -0.8 + EPS:
        t_92 = t
    if t_88 is None and df <= -1.2 + EPS:
        t_88 = t
        break

print("RoCoF0 = %.3f Hz/s" % rocof0)
print("t(49.2 Hz) = %.2f s" % t_92)
print("t(48.8 Hz) = %.2f s" % t_88)

Actual output of this listing, run as printed:

RoCoF0 = -0.125 Hz/s
t(49.2 Hz) = 6.40 s
t(48.8 Hz) = 9.60 s

computed by the listing. The EPS guard exists because 640 additions of the floating-point value -0.00125 land at -0.7999999999999885, about 1.4 parts in 1014 short of the exact -0.8 — without the guard the loop overshoots by one 0.01 s step and prints 6.41 s. The 48.8 Hz threshold has the same problem: 960 additions give -1.19999999999998, and without the guard the loop prints 9.61 s. The physics is not in question here, only the arithmetic: the true crossing is exactly 6.40 s, since RoCoF is a plain constant by assumption in this lesson.

Fig. 1.2. Frequency against time for the opening problem's 1000 MW loss at three values of Ekin,sys: 100, 200, and 400 GVA·s, each a straight line because RoCoF is constant while KD = 0 and no control acts. Times to 49.2 Hz: 3.2, 6.4, 12.8 s. Times to 48.8 Hz: 4.8, 9.6, 19.2 s. Halving the stored energy exactly halves every crossing time; doubling it doubles them — RoCoF is inversely proportional to Ekin,sys, so time-to-threshold is directly proportional to it.

What this model captures, and what it misses

  • No governor or primary frequency response. Real generators change Pm within seconds of a frequency deviation; this lesson holds Pm fixed after the trip. Lesson 8 adds governor response and repeats this same event.
  • No load damping Dload. Real loads (motors, especially) draw less power as frequency falls, which acts like a damping term separate from KD above; this lesson sets it to zero. Lesson 8's exercises restore it.
  • One aggregated mass. Treating the whole grid as a single Ekin,sys assumes every machine's Δω is identical at every instant. Real machines oscillate against each other before settling to a common frequency. Lesson 8 relaxes this into a multi-mass model.
  • No network. This lesson has no buses, lines, or power flow — only one lumped power balance. Lesson 2 introduces the network.

Exercises

1. (Derivational) A 4-pole, 60 Hz, 200 MVA machine has a rotor with J = 5000 kg·m². Compute H.

Answer target

ωm = 2π·60 / 2 = 188.5 rad/s (a 4-pole machine has 2 pole pairs, so mechanical speed = electrical speed / 2 pole pairs); Ekin = ½ J ωm² = 88.8 MJ; H = Ekin / Sbase = 88.8 MJ / 200 MVA = 0.444 s. The value J = 5000 kg·m² is illustrative, not data for a real machine. Typical turbo-set values run 2 s to 8 s cited [S01 — verify], 4.5 to 18 times this result; a real set's H comes from the total J of the generator rotor and the turbine on the same shaft.

2. (Computational) Change E_KIN in the listing to 100e3 and to 400e3 and report the time to 49.2 Hz for each.

Answer target

100e3 → 3.20 s; 400e3 → 12.80 s, matching Fig. 1.2.

3. (Computational) A rate-of-change-of-frequency relay set at 1 Hz/s cited [S16 — verify] trips embedded generation. Find the Ekin,sys below which a 1000 MW loss at 50 Hz trips it.

Answer target

Ekin,sys = f0 ΔP / (2 × 1) = 50 × 1000 / 2 = 25,000 MW·s = 25 GVA·s. Note on sources: two RoCoF figures circulate in the industry and must never be merged. The 1 Hz/s figure used here is the one applied in Ireland and in Great Britain loss-of-mains protection policy cited [S16 — verify] — it is not the ENTSO-E figure, which is a withstand capability of 2 Hz/s measured over 500 ms cited [S15 — verify], a different quantity for a different purpose.

4. (Derivational) Show that H has the unit second, and that H does not change when the machine is described on a different voltage base.

Answer target

Units: Ekin has unit joule = W·s; Sbase has unit W (or VA, dimensionally power); H = Ekin / Sbase has unit W·s / W = s. Voltage base: neither J nor ωm0 in Ekin = ½ J ωm0² depends on any chosen voltage base — they are mechanical quantities — and Sbase is an apparent-power rating, also independent of voltage base by definition (it is the same MVA number whether you describe the machine's terminals in kV or in per unit of any Vbase). So H is unchanged by a voltage-base change; it does change if you change Sbase itself (for example, re-basing from the machine's own rating to a system base), since H is inversely proportional to Sbase.

5. (Conceptual) Two machines on one grid run at 50.0 Hz and 50.1 Hz for one minute. Compute the angle between them after that minute and say why the grid cannot operate that way.

Answer target

The angle accumulates at 2π times the frequency difference: 2π × 0.1 × 60 = 37.7 rad, which is 37.7 / 2π = 6.0 full turns — six complete "slips" of one machine's rotor angle past the other's in one minute. The grid cannot operate this way because the power transferred between two machines connected through a reactance goes as sin(δ), so every time the angle δ sweeps through a multiple of π (180°) the power flow between them reverses sign — 12 reversals in the minute — and every time it passes through ±90° the machine is at its steady-state stability limit; a persistent frequency difference of 0.1 Hz makes the machines slip poles continuously, which is loss of synchronism, not a steady operating state.
